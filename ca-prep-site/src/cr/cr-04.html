What corporate governance is and why it matters

Corporate governance: a set of relationships between a company's management, its board, its shareholders and other stakeholders. It provides the structure through which objectives are set, and the means of attaining them and monitoring performance are determined (OECD, 2015).

In short, governance: manages and reduces risk; divides rights and responsibilities between board, managers, shareholders and stakeholders; sets rules for decisions; and provides the structure for setting and monitoring objectives. Good governance attracts investment (especially in developing countries), supports market confidence and guards against misuse of resources.

Drivers of governance reformExamples
Financial reporting failuresCreative accounting and off-balance-sheet finance eroded confidence
Corporate scandalsPolly Peck, BCCI, Maxwell (1990s); Enron; the 2008–09 crisis; Carillion, Patisserie Valerie, BHS, Sports Direct (leading to the UK Code 2024)
Excessive directors' payPay out of line with performance; shareholder activism (Morrisons 2021: over 70% voted against higher executive pay)
Features of poor governanceRisks that follow
Board dominated by one person (often chair and CEO combined) or a small group; appointments by personal connection; lack of involvement by the board; lack of independent scrutiny (weak NEDs, captured auditors); lack of supervision of staff; no independent internal audit; lack of contact with shareholders; emphasis on short-term profit; misleading accounts and informationFraud and misuse of assets; poor decisions and failed projects; misleading reporting; loss of investor and lender confidence; regulatory action; damaged reputation (e.g. Volkswagen emissions scandal); business failure

Governance concepts

Fairness

Treat all shareholders and stakeholders even-handedly, including minorities.

Openness and transparency

Disclose information openly and clearly, not just what is required.

Independence

Board members and auditors free from influences that compromise judgement.

Probity and honesty

Telling the truth and not misleading.

Responsibility

Management accepts credit or blame for its decisions.

Accountability

Directors answer to shareholders and others for their stewardship.

Reputation

Built on good conduct; lost quickly through scandal.

Judgement

Balanced decisions that improve the company's prosperity.

Integrity

Straightforward dealing and complete, honest information.

Scepticism

Directors and NEDs question management rather than rubber-stamping.

Innovation

Encouraging new ideas within a controlled framework.

Sustainability

Long-term success that considers environment and society.

Bangladesh: BSEC Code and Bangladesh Bank rules

Governance rules in Bangladesh come mainly from BSEC (listed companies: guidelines 2006, Corporate Governance Code 2018, last revised October 2023) and Bangladesh Bank (banks, under the Bank Company Act 1991).

BSEC Code areaMain requirements
1. Board of directorsEffective, balanced board of a suitable size; separate Chair and MD/CEO; clearly defined roles
2. Independent directorsCriteria, qualifications, tenure and duties; at least one-fifth of the board
3. CommitteesAudit Committee and Nomination and Remuneration Committee (NRC), each with terms of reference and reporting duties
4. Internal control and riskSound internal control, risk management and compliance systems
5. Reporting and disclosureTimely, accurate disclosure, including related party transactions and governance reporting (directors' report items in Chapter 1, topic 4)
6. CEO and CFOFormal certification that the accounts are fair and internal controls are effective
7. AuditIndependent external audit, overseen by the Audit Committee; limits on non-audit services by the auditor
8. Compliance reportingA checklist of compliance with every condition, with an independent compliance certificate
Banks: Bangladesh Bank framework
Prudential Regulations for Banks (2009): chapter on corporate governance, dividing duties between board, chair, CEO and management; board oversight, internal control and risk management
BRPD Circular 02 (11 Feb 2024): board formation, fit and proper criteria, board duties · BRPD Circular 03 (14 Feb 2024): independent directors: appointment, qualifications, duties, honorarium · BRPD Circular 05 (Feb 2024): MD/CEO appointment and duties · BRPD Circular Letter 27 (12 May 2021): appointment of directors, advisers and consultants to avoid conflicts
National Integrity Strategy (2012): government anti-corruption framework; Bangladesh Bank requires banks and FIs to set up NIS committees, work plans and quarterly disclosures
Check the detail

The workbook gives the BSEC Code by area only and refers to the Corporate Laws workbook for details. The ratios in the key numbers (one-fifth independent directors, separate Chair and CEO, audit committee of at least 3 chaired by an independent director) are from the BSEC Code 2018. Check the October 2023 revision before quoting exact figures.

Principles of good governance (UK Corporate Governance Code as benchmark)

AreaPrinciples
Board leadership and company purposeAn effective, entrepreneurial board collectively responsible for long-term sustainable success; a culture aligned with purpose, values and strategy; report how decisions served the strategy and explain departures from the Code; engage shareholders and stakeholders; listen to the workforce
Division of responsibilitiesThe chair leads the board and encourages debate; balance of executives and NEDs to avoid dominance; NEDs have time to challenge and support; enough information, time and company secretary support
Composition, succession, evaluationFormal, rigorous, transparent appointments with succession planning and diversity; right mix of skills, refreshed regularly; regular evaluation of the board and each director
Audit, risk and internal controlEffective, independent internal and external audit; fair, balanced reports; a realistic view of prospects (viability); understand principal risks and maintain risk management and internal control
RemunerationPay linked to long-term strategy; transparent, objective procedure; discretion to reflect performance
Key UK Code provisions
Chair and independent NEDs: maximum 9 years · at least half the board (excluding the chair) independent NEDs · a senior independent director · nomination committee with a majority of independent NEDs · annual re-election of all directors · audit committee of at least 3 independent NEDs, at least one with recent and relevant financial experience · audit committee reviews the accounts, internal control, risk management, internal audit and the external auditor (appointment, terms, independence, effectiveness, non-audit work) · remuneration committee of at least 3 independent NEDs · NED fees with no share options or performance pay · executive share awards vest and are held for at least 5 years · notice periods of 1 year or less

UK FRC Audit Committees and the External Audit: Minimum Standard (May 2023): the audit committee ensures access to a competitive audit market and an effective audit; leads auditor tendering (including firms outside the Big Four, choosing on quality not price); oversees the auditor, encouraging challenge and assessing effectiveness; and reports on significant issues, policies, tenure, independence and non-audit services.

Example: British Airways

Since 2021 one person has been both chair and CEO of BA. How can this be acceptable?

BA is owned by International Airlines Group (IAG), which had a strong structure (separate chair, 10 NEDs, 2 executive directors). Always look at the ownership structure before judging governance.

The board and non-executive directors

Board's role
Set strategy and objectives; approve major projects and investments; monitor performance against plans; oversee risk management and internal control; appoint, evaluate and plan succession of senior managers; set values and culture; ensure accurate reporting and dialogue with shareholders. Matters reserved for the board should be listed formally.
Effective boardDirectors with the right skills, independence and time; full, timely information; regular evaluation; clear division between running the board (chair) and running the business (CEO); a strong company secretary.
Non-executive directorsStrategy (contribute and challenge), scrutiny (monitor management's performance), risk (check controls and information are robust), people (set executive pay, appoint and remove executives). Sources: other companies' executives, retired executives, professionals (accountants, lawyers), academics, headhunters. Independence threats: past employment, business relationships, family ties, cross-directorships, long tenure, share options.
Example: SPV (listed pharmaceutical company)

Board: a combined chair/CEO, 3 executives and 2 NEDs. The chair sits on the audit and remuneration committees. NEDs serve two or three 4-year terms. Internal audit reports monthly to the board. A new R&D facility ran late and over budget and may not be value for money; institutional investors may vote against the accounts. One NED says the board just rubber-stamped what R&D managers wanted and threatens to resign.

FailureRecommendation
Board did not approve and monitor the major project properly (rubber stamp)Formal investment appraisal and regular progress reports; matters reserved for the board
Chair and CEO combined: too much power in one personSplit the roles, or appoint a senior independent director
Only 2 NEDs out of 6: not enough independent challengeAppoint more independent NEDs so they are at least half the board
Chair on audit and remuneration committees: not independentCommittees of independent NEDs only
NED terms up to 12 years: independence at riskLimit to 9 years with annual re-election
Internal audit reports to the full board, including executivesReport to the audit committee
Poor relationship with investors and NEDsEngage with institutional investors before the AGM; address NED concerns
Example: Hammond Brothers (preparing to list)

Directors set their own salaries informally; no formal reporting or controls; no internal audit.

Remuneration committee: independent NEDs who set executive pay policy and packages, link pay to long-term performance, avoid directors deciding their own pay, and report to shareholders. It needs independence, clear terms of reference, access to pay data and outside advice. Organisation and management controls: a clear structure and lines of authority; segregation of duties; authorisation limits; budgets and regular management reports; board and committee meetings with minutes; formal policies and procedures; set up an internal audit function and an audit committee.

International impact: OECD and Sarbanes-Oxley

G20/OECD Principles (2023)An effective governance framework; rights and fair treatment of shareholders, including minorities; institutional investors and markets that support good governance; disclosure and transparency; responsibilities of the board; sustainability and resilience. Principles-based ("comply or explain").
Sarbanes-Oxley Act (US, 2002)Rules-based, after Enron and WorldCom. CEO and CFO certify the accounts and internal control (criminal penalties); management reports on internal control over financial reporting, with auditor attestation (s.404); PCAOB oversees auditors; bans many non-audit services to audit clients; audit partner rotation; independent audit committees; whistleblower protection.

Rules vs principles: rules give certainty and are easy to enforce but encourage box-ticking and are costly; principles are flexible and focus on substance but are open to interpretation. Bangladesh's BSEC Code requires compliance with each condition and an independent compliance certificate, so it sits closer to a rules-based approach.

Auditors communicating with those charged with governance

ISA 260 sets what the auditor tells TCWG (responsibilities, planned scope and timing, significant findings, independence for listed entities) and ISA 265 covers significant control deficiencies in writing. Full detail is in Audit and Assurance Chapter 13.

Significant deficiency? Consider
Likelihood of future material misstatement · how open the assets are to fraud · subjectivity and complexity of estimates (fair values) · amounts exposed · volume of activity · importance of the control · cause and frequency of exceptions · interaction with other deficiencies
A report to management should
Not conflict with the audit opinion · say controls were considered only to plan audit procedures, not to give assurance on them · say it covers only deficiencies found and others may exist · be for management's use only · record actions taken and reasons for rejected suggestions · include management's responses where given
Example: to whom does the auditor report first?

(1) The operations manager committed fraud. (2) The finance director committed fraud. (3) Disagreement over an accounting policy. (4) Suspicion that the board is laundering money.

(1) Senior management (for example the finance director), then TCWG. (2) The audit committee or the board excluding the FD, since management is involved. (3) Management first, then TCWG if unresolved. (4) The firm's money laundering reporting officer, who reports to BFIU; do not tell the board (tipping off).

Exam focus

Governance is examined through scenarios: a dominant CEO, weak NEDs, a missing or poorly composed audit committee, executive pay, or a company preparing to list. Link governance weaknesses to audit risk (management override, weak control environment, going concern) and to ISA 260 communications. Practice: SPV, Hammond Brothers.
