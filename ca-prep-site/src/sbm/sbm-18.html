The treasury management function

Treasury management handles the organisation's cash, funding, investments and financial risk. A treasury policy sets objectives, authority limits, approved instruments and counterparties, risk limits, hedging rules, reporting and control.

Role of the treasurerLiquidity and cash management; funding and bank relations; investing surplus cash; managing interest, currency and credit risk; advising on capital structure, dividends and investments; relations with credit rating agencies; insurance.
Advantages of a separate treasury departmentSpecialist expertise; bulk buying power and better terms; centralised cash and netting; consistent risk policy; economies of scale; dedicated control and reporting.
Cost centreProfit centre
Treasury is judged on service quality and cost; deals only to manage the group's risksTreasury trades to earn profit (speculation). Higher return but large risk of loss and fraud; requires strong controls and limits
  • Outsourcing of treasury tasks (cash management, hedging) may cut cost but loses control and knowledge.
  • Control of the treasury: board-approved policy, segregation of duties (front, middle and back office), dealing limits, confirmations, independent valuation and reporting, internal audit, authority levels. Past treasury scandals came from poor controls and unauthorised trading.
  • Current developments: automation and treasury management systems, real-time cash visibility, fintech and payment innovations, treasury analytics, digital currencies, ESG-linked funding.
2

Global treasury management

IssuesSeveral currencies and banks; exchange controls; local tax and regulation; time zones; local cash needs; counterparty risk; differences in interest rates and market access.
StructureCentralised: one treasury makes decisions and runs netting and pooling (control, economies of scale; may lose local knowledge). Decentralised: each unit manages its own (local knowledge; duplication and weak control). Regional centres are a compromise.

Tools: netting of intra-group payments (only the net amount is paid), cash pooling (balances offset in one account), in-house bank, central hedging, leading and lagging. Taxation issues: withholding tax, interest deductibility limits, transfer pricing on intra-group loans, thin capitalisation, tax on pooled balances.

3

Working capital management

Working capital = current assets − current liabilities. Efficient management frees cash, lowers financing cost and supports value creation, but too little working capital risks stock-outs and failure to pay.

Cash operating cycle

Inventory days 60; receivable days 45; payable days 40.

Inventory days = inventory ÷ cost of sales × 36560
Receivable days = receivables ÷ credit sales × 36545
Payable days = payables ÷ cost of sales (or purchases) × 365(40)
Cash operating cycle65 days

The cycle is the time cash is tied up between paying suppliers and collecting from customers. Shorten it to release cash.

Financing working capital and special situations

ApproachFinancingRisk and return
AggressiveUse short-term funds for part of the permanent current assets; low holdings of inventory and cash; tight creditLower cost (short-term debt is cheaper) but higher risk of liquidity trouble and refinancing; may lose sales
ConservativeFund permanent and part of the fluctuating needs with long-term funds; high inventory, cash and liberal creditSafer but more costly and lower returns
Moderate (matching)Fund fluctuating current assets with short-term funds and permanent assets with long-term fundsBalanced
OvertradingExpanding sales faster than working capital funding. Signs: rapid sales growth, falling current and quick ratios, rising payables and overdraft, falling cash, longer supplier credit, tight liquidity while profit looks good. Fix with long-term finance, tighter credit and inventory control, slower growth.
In a recessionCustomers pay later and some fail; banks tighten credit; demand falls. Actions: tighten credit control, cut inventory, negotiate with suppliers, secure facilities early, use invoice finance, protect cash above profit, watch covenants.

Exporters: longer payment cycles, higher credit risk and currency risk; use letters of credit, credit insurance, forfaiting, and hedging (Chapter 16).

Exam focus

Typical tasks: calculate and interpret the cash cycle and recommend actions; evaluate a discount or credit policy change (cost against benefit); advise on financing working capital; diagnose overtrading; design a treasury policy and controls; discuss centralising treasury.
