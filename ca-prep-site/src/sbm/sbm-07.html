Risk, uncertainty and risk appetite

Risk and uncertaintyRisk: the chance that actual results differ from expected, usually with probabilities that can be estimated (it has upside and downside). Uncertainty: the future outcomes and their probabilities are not known.
Risk and businessTaking risk is necessary to earn return. The aim is not to remove risk but to take the right risks within the organisation's appetite, and manage the rest.

Risk appetite is the amount and type of risk the organisation is willing to accept to pursue its objectives. It is shaped by personal views of managers, shareholder demands, organisation culture and national culture. Risk attitude may be risk-averse, risk-neutral or risk-seeking. Boards must agree appetite and tolerance levels and communicate them. Managers may take too little or too much risk because their own interests (bonus, job) differ from the owners'.

Source of riskExamples
StrategicWrong strategy, competitor moves, technology change, reputation, M&A failure
OperationalProcess failure, people, supply chain, IT failure, fraud, safety
FinancialMarket (interest, FX, commodity), credit, liquidity, gearing
ComplianceLaw, regulation, data protection, tax, ethics
Event vs continuousOne-off events (fire, cyber attack) against continuing exposures (price changes)

Business risk and financial risk: business risk comes from the nature of operations and shows in the variability of operating profit (operating gearing: fixed costs). Financial risk comes from debt financing and shows in the variability of earnings to shareholders (financial gearing). Together they make total risk to shareholders; a firm with high business risk should limit gearing.

Reporting consequences

Risk affects the accounts: provisions (IAS 37), impairment, fair values, going concern, and disclosures. Principles for risk reporting in the annual report: describe principal risks and how they are managed, be specific to the company, update every year, link to strategy. Risk assurance gives users confidence in the reporting.

2

Enterprise risk management (ERM)

ERM manages risk across the whole organisation as one integrated process, linked to strategy and performance, rather than in separate silos.

COSO ERM framework: componentContent
Governance and cultureBoard oversight, operating structure, culture, commitment to values, attracting and keeping talent
Strategy and objective-settingUnderstand the business context, define risk appetite, evaluate alternative strategies, set business objectives
PerformanceIdentify, assess and prioritise risks, choose responses, take a portfolio view
Review and revisionAssess substantial change, review performance and risk, improve ERM
Information, communication and reportingUse information and technology, communicate risk information, report on risk, culture and performance
Benefits of ERMBetter decisions with risk in view; fewer surprises and losses; align strategy and appetite; efficient use of capital; clear responsibilities; stronger reputation with investors and regulators.
Risk architecture and guidanceRisk architecture sets out the structure, strategy and protocols for risk: board, committees, risk function, policies, systems. Risk guidance for boards stresses an ongoing process, embedded in business, with a regular review and a viability statement on the company's prospects over a longer period (a UK practice).

Risk resourcing: provide enough people, systems, training and budget for risk management, in proportion to the size of the risks.

Responsibilities and stakeholders

WhoResponsibility
BoardOverall responsibility: set appetite, approve the risk policy, review risk and control at least annually, ensure a sound system
Risk committeeDetailed review of risk exposure, policy and reports (in financial institutions it is often mandatory)
Management and risk managerDay-to-day risk management; risk register; reporting up
All employeesFollow controls; report incidents; risk awareness
Internal auditIndependent assurance that risk processes and controls work (third line)

Embedding risk awareness: include risk in strategy and budgets, HR recruitment, appraisal and rewards, training, a risk policy statement, clear reporting lines and a no-blame incident culture. Control systems (preventive, detective, corrective) are the main way risks are kept within appetite.

How stakeholders view riskShareholders: want return for risk and can diversify. Lenders: want security and safe cash flows. Employees: job security and safety. Customers and suppliers: continuity and quality. Community: environment, safety.
Why it mattersStakeholders with power can react to risk (investors sell, lenders raise rates, regulators fine, customers leave). Risk strategy must be acceptable to them.

KPIs, review, update the register

The risk register records each risk with description, cause, owner, likelihood, impact, existing controls, residual risk and action. Profiling plots risks on a likelihood-impact grid: high-high risks need immediate action; low-low can be accepted. Consolidation adds up risks across units to see the total exposure and diversification effects.

Quantifying risk: statistics used in the exam

Expected value and standard deviation

A project's contribution: £2,000 (probability 0.2), £5,000 (0.5), £9,000 (0.3).

Outcome × probability£
2,000 × 0.2400
5,000 × 0.52,500
9,000 × 0.32,700
Expected value (EV)5,600
Variance = Σ p × (x − EV)²
0.2 × (2,000 − 5,600)² = 0.2 × 12,960,0002,592,000
0.5 × (5,000 − 5,600)² = 0.5 × 360,000180,000
0.3 × (9,000 − 5,600)² = 0.3 × 11,560,0003,468,000
Variance 6,240,000; standard deviation √6,240,0002,498

Coefficient of variation = SD ÷ EV = 2,498 ÷ 5,600 = 0.45: risk per unit of return. Use it to compare projects with different EVs. A higher SD means more risk. Remember that EV is only an average: the single project will never produce exactly EV, and it ignores the attitude to risk.

TechniqueUse
Mean, standard deviationCentral value and spread of past data (sample SD uses n − 1)
Normal distributionAbout 68% of values lie within ±1 SD of the mean, 95% within ±1.96 SD (use z = (x − mean) ÷ SD and tables) to find the probability of a loss or a target
Hypothesis testingTest whether a sample mean differs from a claim: z = (x̄ − μ) ÷ (σ ÷ √n)
Regression y = a + bxFit a line to past data to forecast (b is the slope, a the intercept)

DiversificationCombining assets or activities that are not perfectly correlated reduces total risk. Only unsystematic (specific) risk can be diversified

Risk response, monitoring and reporting

ResponseMeaningExample
TransferPass the risk to another partyInsurance, hedging, outsourcing, contract terms
Avoid (terminate)Stop the activityDo not enter a high-risk market; sell a unit
Reduce (treat)Lower likelihood or impactControls, training, backups, diversification, contingency plans
Accept (tolerate)Live with the risk if within appetite and the cost of response is higher than the benefitSmall risks; risks with no cost-effective response
  • Risk pooling and diversification spread risk (insurers, portfolios, multiple suppliers).
  • Implementation: assign owners, set budgets and dates, define key risk indicators. Monitoring: regular review by management, internal audit and the board; track incidents and near misses.
  • Reporting: internal risk reports (to board and committees) and external reporting on principal risks and uncertainties.
  • Limitations: human error and collusion, poor judgement about likelihood, cost against benefit, new unforeseen risks, management override, reliance on past data.

ESG and climate risk management

Nature of ESG risksEnvironmental (climate, pollution, resource scarcity), social (labour conditions, human rights, safety, community) and governance (corruption, board failure). They can be strategic, operational, financial and reputational, and often build over time.
Climate riskPhysical: acute (floods, cyclones) or chronic (rising seas, heat). Transition: new laws, carbon pricing, technology change, shifting customer preferences, litigation. Highly relevant for coastal and low-lying countries such as Bangladesh.

Include ESG in the ERM framework: identify and assess exposures, set targets, assign owners, report using TCFD or IFRS S2 style disclosure, and seek assurance. Consider scenario analysis (for example a 2°C and a 4°C world) to test strategy.

Exam focus

Typical tasks: identify and assess risks in a scenario (risk register with likelihood, impact, response); advise the board on its risk responsibilities and committees; calculate expected value and standard deviation and interpret; recommend a response (transfer, avoid, reduce, accept); and discuss ESG risk.
