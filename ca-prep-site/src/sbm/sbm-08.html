Data Analysis

How to judge the quality of data, analyse strategic, financial and operational information, use big data, AI and trend analysis, present data, ask for more information, give assurance, and, most important for the exam, how to turn numbers into meaningful analysis with the WHAT–HOW–WHY–WHEN–SO WHAT method.

"Because"The one word that turns a fact into analysis
1

Data and its quality

Data analysis starts with asking: what decision is this data for, and can I trust it? Judge data by its characteristics.

CharacteristicQuestion to ask
RelevanceDoes it help the decision?
Accuracy and completenessAre there errors or gaps?
TimelinessIs it up to date?
Consistency and comparabilitySame definitions across years, units and sources?
Source and biasWho produced it and why (management forecasts, surveys, social media)?
Sample and sizeIs it representative enough?
Cost versus benefitIs collecting more worth the cost?

Apply professional scepticism: look for gaps, inconsistencies with other sources and unusual patterns. Assurance can raise confidence in data and KPIs (see section 7).

2

Strategic, financial and operational data

LevelReports and what to analyse
StrategicMarket and competitor data, forecasts, PESTEL, industry analysis, risks, strengths and weaknesses (Chapter 1)
FinancialIncome statement, balance sheet, cash flow, ratios, trends, segment data, cost of capital
OperationalEfficiency and effectiveness (output per hour, defect rates, capacity use), balanced scorecard, cost data
Financial analysisUsers: investors, lenders, management, employees, regulators. Look at profitability, liquidity, gearing, efficiency, investor ratios, trends and cash flow. Adjust the reported figures first if needed: remeasure to market value, recognise missing assets or liabilities, strip out one-off items, spot creative accounting (income smoothing, understated provisions).
Sustainability reportsUsers (investors, communities, customers) need consistent, comparable, reliable ESG data. Consider the framework used, the scope, the quality of data, whether it is assured and whether the story matches the financial results.

The exam method: turn numbers into analysis

The manual says the biggest weakness in Advanced Level answers is a lack of meaningful analysis. Use the five steps and the five questions.

QuestionMeaningExample
WHATWhat happened overall?Revenue rose 21%
HOWHow did it happen (the components)?Price rose 10% and volume rose 10%
WHYWhy? The underlying cause in the scenarioNew features justified a higher price and also raised demand

The "because" test

A good answer passes the "because" test. Weak: "Market share fell from 35% to 29%." Strong: "Market share fell from 35% to 29% because a new low-cost competitor entered and our prices stayed 12% higher."

Weakness in answersFix
Restating numbers without explainingAdd "because" and tie to the scenario
Generic points that could fit any companyUse names, products, numbers from the scenario
Looking at each figure aloneLink figures: rising share but falling margin suggests price cutting
Only financial measuresUse balanced-scorecard headings for a wider view
Not using the analysis later in the answer"Make the numbers talk" in the recommendation (a loss-making business cannot do nothing)
No cause and effectBreak figures down: sales per outlet, price × volume × mix
Imbalance of calculation and descriptionDo a table first (or alongside), then interpret; watch the time
Treating IFRS profit as the whole truthStrategy changes may reduce profit in the short term; adjust for one-offs
Example of breaking a number down

Sales grew 10% a year but the number of outlets grew 15% a year.

Sales per outlet is falling about 4% a year (1.10 ÷ 1.15 = 0.957). So growth comes from opening more outlets, not from better performance in existing outlets, which may mean weaker new locations or cannibalisation.

4

Big data, analytics and AI

Big dataData too large, fast or varied for normal tools: volume, velocity, variety, plus veracity (quality) and value. Sources: transactions, sensors (internet of things), web and social media, customer records.
Analytics typesDescriptive (what happened), diagnostic (why), predictive (what will happen), prescriptive (what to do).
  • Uses: customer insight and personalisation, pricing, demand forecasting, fraud detection, supply chain efficiency, risk models, predictive maintenance, new products.
  • AI and machine learning: learn patterns from data to predict or classify. They can automate decisions.
  • Limits and risks: poor-quality or biased data, privacy and consent, security, cost and skills, over-reliance, wrong conclusions from correlation, "black box" models, regulation.
  • Ethics and governance: clear ownership of data, consent, fair use, explainable decisions, human oversight, data protection compliance.
  • Reaching wrong conclusions: correlation is not causation; small or unrepresentative samples; ignoring outliers or seasonality; selecting only supporting evidence.
5

Trend analysis and data visualisation

TechniqueHow it works
Moving averageAverage of the last n periods smooths seasonal and random variation to show the trend
Linear regression (least squares)y = a + bx; b = slope (change per period), a = intercept. In a spreadsheet use TREND or FORECAST.LINEAR to extend the line
Time series modelActual = trend + seasonal variation + random. Forecast = projected trend + average seasonal factor (additive) or × factor (multiplicative)
Index numbers and growth ratesCompare changes against a base year; compound annual growth = (end ÷ start)^(1/years) − 1

Limits of forecasts: the past may not continue; few data points; structural change; external shocks. Data visualisation (line, bar, scatter, heat map, dashboard) makes patterns clear: choose the chart that fits the message, label axes, avoid distortion and clutter.

6

Obtaining more information

Data is never complete. In an answer, list the additional information you would ask for and why. Examples: segment profits by product and region; customer retention and satisfaction; competitor prices and share; breakdown of costs (fixed and variable); cash flow forecasts; details of one-off items; sources and method of forecasts; industry benchmarks; ESG data and targets.

Limitations of available data include small samples, old data, a single source, definition changes and management bias.

7

Assurance engagements

TypeLevel and output
Reasonable assurance (for example an audit)High but not absolute; positive conclusion ("gives a true and fair view")
Limited assurance (for example a review)Lower; negative conclusion ("nothing has come to our attention")
Agreed-upon proceduresThe practitioner performs procedures the parties agree and reports factual findings, with no assurance conclusion; users form their own conclusion

Five elements of an assurance engagement: three parties (practitioner, responsible party, intended users), an appropriate subject matter, suitable criteria, sufficient appropriate evidence and a written report. Non-financial information (ESG, KPIs) can be assured if the criteria are clear and data is measurable. In the SBM exam assurance is a recurring theme: suggest what assurance would help, its scope and its limits.

Exam focus

Expect a data table (sales by region, ratios, KPIs) and a requirement to analyse and advise. Do the calculation, apply WHAT–HOW–WHY–WHEN–SO WHAT, link to the strategy, name extra information needed and say what assurance is possible.
