1

Why hedge accounting, and the three types

Without hedge accounting, a derivative is at FVTPL while the item it protects may be at cost, or not recognised yet, so profit swings even though the risk is covered. Hedge accounting is optional and lets the gains and losses offset in the same period. It is meant to reflect the entity's risk management.

TypeHedgesTypical example
Fair value hedgeChanges in fair value of a recognised asset or liability or a firm commitmentSelling gold futures to protect inventory value; swapping fixed-rate debt to floating
Cash flow hedgeVariability in cash flows of a recognised item or a highly probable forecast transactionForward contract for a planned foreign-currency purchase; swapping floating-rate debt to fixed
Net investment hedgeCurrency risk on a net investment in a foreign operationForeign-currency loan held by the parent against an overseas subsidiary

A firm commitment's currency risk may be treated as either a fair value or a cash flow hedge.

2

Hedged items and hedging instruments

Hedged itemsRecognised assets and liabilities, firm commitments, highly probable forecast transactions, net investments in foreign operations. Can be a component (e.g. the crude oil element of jet fuel, if separately identifiable and reliably measurable), a group of similar items, an aggregated exposure (an item plus a derivative), or a layer. Equity investments at FVOCI can be hedged (effects stay in OCI). Intra-group items only in group accounts if they create FX gains or losses that are not eliminated (e.g. intra-group monetary items in different currencies, or a highly probable intra-group forecast transaction).
Hedging instrumentsDerivatives at FVTPL (except most written options), and non-derivative financial assets or liabilities at FVTPL. For currency risk only, a non-derivative financial asset or liability (e.g. a foreign-currency loan) can be used. Usually designated in full, but the time value of options, the forward element of forwards and currency basis spreads can be excluded and treated as a cost of hedging through OCI.

Highly probable forecast transactions: consider past frequency of similar transactions, ability and intention to carry them out, the length of time to the transaction, and the size compared with normal activity.

3

Qualifying conditions, hedge ratio and rebalancing

1 Only eligible items and instruments 2 Formal designation and documentation at inception: risk objective, items, risk, how effectiveness is assessed, hedge ratio 3 Effectiveness: economic relationship; credit risk does not dominate; hedge ratio matches what risk management actually uses

Hedge ratio: quantity of instrument to quantity of item. CoffeeBar hedges 1,700 lbs of part-processed coffee with 1,000 lbs of commodity forwards because the market price is 1.7 times its raw price, so the ratio is 1 : 1.7. Rebalancing: if the risk objective is unchanged but the ratio no longer works, adjust the quantity of item or instrument (when the relationship moves to 1.3 times, reset to 1 : 1.3). If the risk objective changes, discontinue.

Discontinuing

Stop hedge accounting only when the relationship no longer qualifies after any rebalancing (e.g. instrument sold or expired, risk objective changed, economic relationship gone). Voluntary de-designation is not allowed while the risk objective is unchanged.

4

Fair value hedges

Instrument: gain or loss → profit or loss. Hedged item: carrying amount adjusted for the hedged risk → profit or loss (OCI if the item is an FVOCI equity investment). The two offset; any difference is ineffectiveness.
Worked example: jewellery trader hedging inventory

10,000 oz bought at £200 (£2m). Sold futures at £210 for delivery in 12 months. At year end: spot £220, futures £227. At settlement: £230; inventory sold and futures closed out.

Year end: futures loss 10,000 × (227 − 210) (P/L, liability)(£170,000)
Year end: inventory up 10,000 × (220 − 200) (P/L)£200,000
Settlement: further futures loss 10,000 × 3; inventory gain 10,000 × 10(£30,000) / £100,000
Sale: revenue £2.3m; cost of sales £2.3m (adjusted inventory); pay £200,000 on futuresNet
Total profit (300,000 gain − 200,000 futures loss)£100,000

Firm commitments (unrecognised): the cumulative change in fair value for the hedged risk is recognised as an asset or liability, and later adjusts the initial carrying amount of the asset or liability acquired. Hedged debt at amortised cost: the adjustment is amortised to profit or loss from when it starts, or no later than when hedge accounting stops.

5

Cash flow hedges and net investment hedges

Effective part of the instrument's gain or loss → OCI (cash flow hedge reserve), limited to the lower of the cumulative gain or loss on the instrument and the cumulative change in the hedged cash flows. The rest (ineffective part) → profit or loss.
Reclassifying the reserveIf the hedged transaction results in a non-financial asset or liability (inventory, PPE), remove the reserve and include it in the asset's initial cost ("basis adjustment"; not through OCI). Otherwise, reclassify to profit or loss when the hedged cash flows affect profit (e.g. as interest is paid). If a loss in the reserve is not expected to be recovered, reclassify it immediately.
When hedging stopsIf the forecast transaction is still expected: keep the reserve until it happens. If it is no longer expected: reclassify to profit or loss at once.
Worked example: Bets Co (gold jewellery)

Sold gold futures for 24,000 oz at £388. Ten months later the forward price is £352. Expected sale value of jewellery fell from £9.938m to £9.186m.

Gain on futures: 24,000 × (388 − 352)£864,000
Change in hedged cash flows: 9.938m − 9.186m£752,000
Lower amount to OCI (cash flow hedge reserve)£752,000
Ineffective part to P/L£112,000
Rebalance: cut futures to 752/864 × 24,000 oz, or increase hedged item to 27,574 oz20,889 oz

Net investment hedges: account like a cash flow hedge. Effective part to OCI (alongside the IAS 21 translation reserve), reclassified to profit or loss on disposal of the foreign operation. A foreign-currency loan can be the instrument.

Entities may keep IAS 39 hedge accounting until the IASB finishes its macro hedging (dynamic risk management) project. Disclosures (IFRS 7): risk management strategy; amount, timing and uncertainty of future cash flows; effects on position and performance (instrument amounts, hedge adjustments, reserve movements, ineffectiveness).

7

Auditing fair values and financial instruments

Fair values (ISA 540)Understand the entity, controls and models. Inherent risk: complexity, subjectivity, market changes. Control risk: model governance, use of experts for Level 3. Challenge management's range and assumptions (scepticism vs bias); check segregation where staff are paid bonuses on valuations; use subsequent events; check disclosures of uncertainty.
Why complex instruments are hard (IAPN 1000)Hard to understand the instrument and risks; fast-moving markets; little valuation evidence; large single payments (misappropriation risk); small carrying amounts hiding large exposures; a few traders with bonus-driven influence.

Planning: understand the reporting requirements, the instruments and their purpose (hedging or speculation), and the control environment (policies, limits, independent price verification, segregation between front office and back office). Valuation tests: use independent, ideally several, price sources; test the validity of models and inputs; consider an auditor's expert; evaluate adjustments and reserves for uncertainty. Financial instruments can justify an engagement quality review even for non-listed clients.

Interactive question: Berriman convertible debenture

£10m 4% debenture, redeemable in 5 years or convertible into 6m shares; all shown as a liability.

Issue: a compound instrument; split into liability (PV at the market rate for debt without conversion) and equity (the rest). Procedures: read the deed for rate and conversion terms; review the split calculation and the discount rate used; agree proceeds and interest to bank; check disclosures.

Auditing derivatives

What goes wrong: weak controls, hands-off management, greed, poor risk monitoring, inexperience. Typical process: trader's deal sheet → system entry → back office reports and processing using market quotes → confirmation with brokers or counterparties → settlement → accounting.

ProcessingBack office report agrees to the deal sheet

Exam focus

Hedging questions ask: does it qualify (documentation at inception, effectiveness)? Which type? Then the numbers: where each gain or loss goes (P/L or OCI), the "lower of" test for cash flow hedges, and ineffectiveness. A frequent trap is hedging designated after the derivative was taken out, with no documentation: then there is no hedge accounting and the derivative is simply FVTPL. Finish with audit risk (valuation, completeness of derivatives, speculation disguised as hedging).
