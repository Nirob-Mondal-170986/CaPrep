1

Responsibilities for internal control

Internal control: a process designed, implemented and maintained by TCWG, management and staff to give reasonable assurance on reliable financial reporting, effective and efficient operations, and compliance with laws and regulations.
Those charged with governanceResponsible for designing, implementing and maintaining internal control that can prevent, or detect and correct, errors. Errors are unintentional misstatements, including omissions.
The auditorUnderstand the controls relevant to the audit; evaluate their design; check they are implemented (ISA 315). Test them when planning to rely on them or when substantive procedures alone are not enough (ISA 330). Report significant deficiencies to TCWG in writing (ISA 265).
2

Evaluating and testing controls

1 Identify controls relevant to the audit 2 Evaluate design and check implementation 3 Well designed: rely and test, or go substantive (whichever is more efficient) 4 Poorly designed: more substantive work; record the deficiency

Five components (ISA 315 Appendix 3): control environment; risk assessment process; process to monitor internal control; information system and communication; control activities.

Tests of controlsInspect evidence of the control (authorisation signatures) · ask who actually does each job · reperform (bank reconciliation) · read management minutes · test IT controls (access) · observe. Show the control worked throughout the period and consistently (different staff, locations, and before and after changes).
After testingThe achieved control risk plus assessed inherent risk gives the detection risk left, which sets the nature, timing and extent of substantive work. Risk assessment procedures (e.g. management's monthly variance review) can sometimes give evidence that controls operate. Automated controls need less testing once general IT controls are sound.
3

Service organisations (ISA 402)

Service organisation: a third party providing services to user entities that form part of their information systems relevant to financial reporting (e.g. payroll bureau, outsourced bookkeeping, asset management, agents).

The user auditor must understand: the nature of the services; the nature and materiality of the transactions affected; how the two organisations' activities interact; the contract terms; and whether accounting records kept outside affect legal reporting duties. Evaluate the user entity's own controls over the service first. If that is not enough: get a service auditor's report, contact the service organisation (through the client), visit it, or use another auditor.

ReportCoversUse to the user auditor
Type 1Description of the system and controls, and suitability of their design, at a dateHelps understanding only; cannot reduce control risk
Type 2As Type 1, plus operating effectiveness over a period, with the tests performed and their resultsCan support a lower control risk if the controls tested are relevant and the tests adequate

Before relying on a report: the service auditor's competence and independence; the standards used; whether the period fits; whether the report is sufficient; and whether the client has the complementary user controls that the service assumes.

Substantive options: inspect records at the client or at the service organisation (contract access rights); confirm balances with the service organisation; analytical procedures on its reports. Opinion: modify if the auditor cannot understand the service, cannot get evidence on controls it planned to rely on, or cannot access records. Never refer to the service auditor in an unmodified report unless the law requires it.

Cloud computing and virtual arrangements

Backups

Regular provider backups? Client's own backups? Restores tested? Service level agreement on data?

Security

Third-party penetration testing? Breach process? Encrypted data? PCI-DSS for card data? ISO accreditation? Protection from denial-of-service attacks?

Loss

Compensation for data loss written into the contract?

Service

Availability and performance commitments; realistic development plan; integration; who provides support.

A cloud provider holding accounting data is a service organisation, so ISA 402 applies. Technology has made audit tools cheaper for smaller firms, and AI moves auditors from routine testing (journals) towards judgement, but blockchain code and AI outputs still need skilled review.

Cyber security and data protection

Threat sourcesCyber criminals · hacktivists · nation states · insiders and partners · competitors · skilled lone hackers. AI now powers both attacks (convincing phishing, scanning for weak points) and defences.
ConsequencesTheft of IP and plans · financial fraud · reputational damage · business disruption · damage to critical infrastructure · health and safety threats · fines and claims (provisions, going concern).
Data protectionKey points
GDPR / UK GDPR (2018)Collect only data with a valid business need; store securely and delete when no longer needed; controllers and processors both liable; Data Protection Impact Assessment for high-risk processing; report breaches; fines up to €20m or 4% of global turnover. Rights: be informed, access, rectification, erasure, restrict processing, portability, object, and rights over automated decisions
ExamplesTikTok fined £12.7m (2023) for under-13 users without parental consent; Ministry of Defence £350k for using "To" not "BCC"; PSNI £750k for publishing staff data
BangladeshConstitution protects privacy of home and correspondence, but there is no full GDPR-style law yet. Cyber Security Act 2023 targets cybercrime and critical infrastructure. A Personal Data Protection law (lawful processing, consent, individual rights, controller and processor duties, cross-border rules) is being introduced
Check current law

Bangladesh's cyber and data laws changed quickly in 2024–25: the Cyber Security Act 2023 was replaced by an ordinance, and a Personal Data Protection Ordinance was being finalised. Check which law is in force before naming it in an answer. The principles above do not change.

Governance: the board is ultimately responsible (often through a chief information security officer); the audit committee oversees, sets expectations and checks resources; internal audit evaluates cyber risk management and controls. Three lines of defence: (1) operational controls by local management; (2) specialist oversight (security team, CIO); (3) internal audit or independent assurance. Supply chain risk: many breaches come through suppliers, so assess by risk, not just by spend, and across the whole relationship. Benefits beyond fewer losses: stronger brand, access to supply-chain contracts, lower insurance premiums.

Audit implications of cyber risk

Strong control environmentBoard-level responsibility with a named person · active board oversight, key data and risks identified · policies embedded and monitored · defined risk appetite · NEDs and audit committee with the right skills · competent in-house staff or consultants · IT talks to the board, internal audit and audit committee · staff can report breaches without fear.
Control procedures: secure, vigilant, resilientPerimeter defences · vulnerability, asset and identity management · data protection · threat intelligence sharing · security monitoring and behaviour analysis · risk analytics · incident response and forensics · business continuity, disaster recovery and crisis management. IT audit specialists usually needed.

Board checklist questions the auditor can borrow: Are we ready for the worst breach? What do we know about the threats? How can critical data actually be reached and by whom? How do strategic changes and acquisitions change the risk? What happens to staff who break the rules? Does the board follow the same rules? How do we spot insider risk? Is security designed in from the start?

Reporting control deficiencies (ISA 265)

DeficiencyA control is designed, implemented or operated so that it cannot prevent, or detect and correct, misstatements on time, or a necessary control is missing.
Significant deficiencyOne that, in the auditor's judgement, is important enough to merit TCWG's attention. Report in writing, on time, to TCWG; other deficiencies go to management. See Chapter 4, topic 7 for the factors and the content of the letter.
Exam focus

Typical requirements: "evaluate the controls and recommend improvements" or "explain whether the auditor can rely on the directors' control review" (Dodgy Burgers: controls brought in part-way through the year, or not yet fully rolled out, cannot be relied on for the whole period). Use a three-column answer: deficiency → consequence → recommendation. For outsourcing, always ask whether a Type 2 report covers the right period and controls. For IT and cyber, separate general from application controls.
