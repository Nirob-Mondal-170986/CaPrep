1

The five-step model

Core principle: recognise revenue to show the transfer of promised goods or services to customers, at the amount the entity expects to be entitled to in exchange.
1. ContractApproved, rights and payment terms identifiable, commercial substance, collection probable
2. Performance obligationsEach distinct good or service (or series)
3. Transaction priceFixed + variable; excludes amounts collected for third parties
4. AllocateIn proportion to stand-alone selling prices
5. RecogniseWhen (or as) control passes: point in time or over time

Distinct = the customer can benefit from it on its own or with readily available resources, and it is separately identifiable in the contract (not integrated into a combined output, not modifying another item, not highly interrelated).

Worked examples: one obligation or three?

(1) Colossal Construction designs, prepares the site, buys materials, builds, plumbs, wires and finishes a warehouse. (2) MetaConnect sells a software licence, standard installation (others could do it) and 4 years of support for £6,000. Stand-alone prices: licence £5,000, installation £1,500, support £3,000.

(1) Inputs integrated into one outputOne obligation
(2) Each is distinct: three obligations. Licence 5,000/9,500 × 6,000£3,158
Installation 1,500/9,500 × 6,000£947
Support 3,000/9,500 × 6,000 (over 4 years)£1,895

Transaction price and allocation

Variable considerationDiscounts, refunds, penalties, bonuses. Estimate by expected value (many similar contracts) or most likely amount (two outcomes, e.g. bonus or no bonus). Include only to the extent it is highly probable there will be no significant reversal. Reversal is more likely if: outside factors (weather, markets), a long wait to resolve, little experience, a habit of price concessions, or many possible outcomes.
Estimating stand-alone priceObservable price first. Otherwise: adjusted market assessment; expected cost plus margin; residual only when the price is highly variable or uncertain. Spread any discount across all obligations unless it clearly relates to one.
Worked example: Danmar Construction (late-completion penalty)

Price £80m, less £200,000 per day late. Chance of: on time 80%, 1 day late 10%, 2 days 7%, 3 days 3%.

80% × 80.0m + 10% × 79.8m + 7% × 79.6m + 3% × 79.4m£79,934,000
Bundles: free handset, car package, Computer Ltd

(a) Free handset (SSP £52) with a 2-year contract at £20 a month. (b) Car + fuel + servicing sold for £27,000; fair values £28,000, £1,200, £800. (c) System invoiced at £800,000 on the last day of the year, with 2 years of support costing £35,000 a year at a 17.5% margin; system sold alone for £775,000.

(a) Total £480; handset 52/532 ≈ 10% → £48 on day one; network £432 over 2 yearsYear 1: £264
(b) 10% discount spread: car £25,200 on delivery; fuel £1,080 over 12 months; servicing £720£27,000
(c) Support SSP = 2 × 35,000 ÷ 82.5% = 84,848; system share 775,000/859,848 × 800,000£720,800 now
Check the method

The workbook answers the same "free support" question two ways: by relative stand-alone selling price (Computer Ltd £720,800; equipment £1,368,000 + £22,000) and by the residual method (£715,152; £1,350,000 + £25,000). IFRS 15 requires the relative stand-alone selling price method. Use the residual approach only when a stand-alone price is highly variable or uncertain. Here the system's price (£775,000) is observable, so use the relative method, and state the method you used.

Recognising revenue: point in time vs over time

Over time if any one applies(1) The customer receives and consumes the benefit as the entity performs (services). (2) The entity's work creates or enhances an asset the customer controls. (3) The asset has no alternative use to the entity and there is an enforceable right to payment for work done to date. Measure progress by output (work certified, milestones) or input (costs incurred ÷ total costs) methods.
Otherwise at a point in timeWhen control passes. Indicators: present right to payment; legal title; physical possession; significant risks and rewards; customer acceptance. If the outcome cannot yet be measured, recognise revenue only up to recoverable costs.
Balance sheet itemWhen
Contract liabilityCustomer paid (or payment due) before the entity performs
Contract assetEntity performed, but the right to payment is conditional on something other than time (not yet invoiced)
ReceivableRight to payment is unconditional, only time must pass (invoiced)
Worked example: Frizco Construction (leisure centre)

Price £70m, less £6m if a month or more late (on schedule now). Costs to date £18.6m, including £1m of wrong-grade material that cannot be used. Total expected costs £44m (excluding the waste). Work certified £24.5m.

Transaction price (most likely: on time)£70m
Output method: 24.5 ÷ 70 = 35% × £70m£24.5m
Input method: (18.6 − 1.0) ÷ 44 = 40% × £70m£28.0m
The £1m waste is expensed and excluded from progress£1m expense

Financing components, contract changes and contract costs

Significant financing component: where payment and delivery are more than about a year apart, adjust for the time value of money using the customer's credit rate (or the rate that discounts to the cash price). Revenue = cash price at delivery; the rest is interest over the credit period. Advance payments create interest expense on the contract liability.

Worked examples: car retailer, Wharfe Boats and the Caravan

(1) Car sold 1 Jan for £20,000: 20% deposit, balance in 2 years; 10% finance rate. (2) Wharfe receives £250,000 on 31 Aug 20X7 for a boat delivered 1 Sept 20X8; borrowing rate 10%. (3) Caravan: £3,000 deposit, £15,000 on delivery (1 Aug 20X7), £12,000 two years later; discount rate 10%; caravan SSP £30,000, free equipment £1,500.

(1) PV of £16,000 = 16,000 ÷ 1.1² = 13,223; revenue 4,000 + 13,223£17,223
Interest income year 1: 13,223 × 10%; receivable at year end £14,545£1,322
(2) Interest expense £25,000 added to the contract liability; revenue on delivery£275,000
(3) Price = 3,000 + 15,000 + 12,000 ÷ 1.1² (= 9,917)£27,917
Allocate: caravan 30/31.5 = £26,588; equipment £1,329; both on 1 Aug 20X7£27,917
Contract modificationTreatment
Adds distinct goods at their stand-alone priceSeparate contract (e.g. 20 extra items at £330 each: original 100 stay at £350)
Remaining goods distinct, but price not at stand-aloneEnd the old contract and start a new one: unrecognised price + new price spread over what is left
Remaining goods not distinct (one combined obligation)Part of the existing contract: catch-up adjustment to revenue and progress

Contract costs: incremental costs of obtaining a contract (sales commission) are an asset if expected to be recovered. Fulfilment costs not covered by another standard are an asset if they relate directly to the contract, create resources used later, and are recoverable. Amortise over the expected customer life, e.g. Copyquick's £35,000 commission ÷ 7 years (5-year contract usually renewed for 2 more) = £5,000 a year.

Special situations

SituationRule
Right of returnRevenue for goods not expected back; refund liability for expected returns; asset for the right to recover goods (cost less expected loss of value), shown separately
WarrantiesAssurance warranty (legally required, short, just "works as promised") → IAS 37 provision. Service warranty (sold separately, long, extra services) → separate obligation
Principal vs agentPrincipal if it controls the good before transfer → gross revenue. Agent signs: another party fulfils, no inventory risk, no pricing discretion, paid by commission, no credit risk → revenue = commission only
LicencesRight to access IP that changes over the licence (franchise with ongoing support and advertising) → over time. Right to use IP as it is at grant → point in time. Sales or usage royalties: recognise at the later of the sale or usage and satisfying the obligation
Repurchase agreementsForward or call option: no control passes → lease if the repurchase price is below the sale price, financing if equal or above. Put option: financing if the customer has a significant incentive to exercise; otherwise a sale with a right of return
ConsignmentDealer has no control (seller controls until sold, can recall, dealer need not pay) → no revenue on delivery to the dealer
Bill and holdRevenue before delivery only if: a substantive reason (customer asked), goods identified as the customer's, ready to transfer, and cannot be used or sent elsewhere. Consider a separate storage service
Customer options and loyaltyA material right (discount only available by buying) is a separate obligation: allocate by stand-alone price × expected take-up; recognise when used or expired. No discount = marketing offer
Non-refundable upfront feesUsually an advance for future services → recognise as those services are provided

Auditing revenue

Revenue is presumed to carry a fraud risk (ISA 240) and is often a KAM. Use the five steps to structure the procedures.

StepProcedures
1. ContractInspect contracts: legally binding, in force this year; for implied (retail) contracts, establish the usual terms
2. ObligationsIdentify the goods and services promised; check whether they are distinct or bundled
3. PriceAgree to the contract; recalculate variable amounts; test the "highly probable" assumptions; check financing components and the rate used
4. AllocationAgree stand-alone prices to price lists; test the assumptions in estimated prices
5. RecognitionPoint in time: delivery evidence and cut-off. Over time: see below
OtherReturns after year end; service warranties; amounts collected for third parties (agent); consignment indicators; bill and hold conditions with customer confirmation; upfront fees; loyalty points used and expected take-up

Contracts over time: agree price and conditional amounts to the contract; confirm progress (surveyors' or customer certificates, site visits); agree costs to invoices and management accounts; exclude wasted or unrelated costs; review estimated costs to complete (budgets, minutes) for overruns and onerous contracts.

Interactive question: Construction Co (office block)

Price £600,000 + £40,000 bonus if on time; 36-month build started 1 March; estimated costs up from £470,000 to £570,000 to fix faults; "30% complete"; revenue recognised £192,000 (30% × £640,000).

Issues: is the bonus highly probable given the faults? Where did 30% come from? By time it is only 10/36 = 27.8%. Rectification costs should be excluded from an input measure. More faults may appear. Margin has fallen from £170k to £70k. Procedures: agree price and bonus terms; ask about the basis of 30% and assess any surveyor; look at the track record for finishing on time; get a schedule of rectification costs with support; challenge cost-to-complete assumptions; recalculate revenue under IFRS 15.

Exam focus

IFRS 15 is in most CR papers (12–20 marks), often combined with audit: explain the right treatment, show the numbers and the correcting entry, then give procedures and say whether it looks like management bias (early recognition, bill and hold, gross vs net). Remember Carillion: aggressive contract revenue and IFRS 15 adoption cut reserves by about £150m before the collapse.
