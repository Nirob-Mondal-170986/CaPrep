Tailor the analysis to the user named in the question: irrelevant ratios hide the relevant ones. In CR the usual viewpoints are the investor and the credit analyst or lender.

Gearing effect: high gearing boosts returns to shareholders when profits rise and hurts them when profits fall, because interest is fixed. Watch the figures used: year-end balances can be untypical (a toy retailer's inventory on 31 December is at its lowest); averages of two unusual dates do not fix this.

3

Interpreting cash flows

SectionWhat to look for
OperatingCompare with operating profit (add back depreciation). Much lower or negative → liquidity danger; causes: inventory build-up during expansion, falling sales or margins, slow collection. Interest and tax are non-discretionary; operating cash must cover interest
InvestingDiscretionary in the short term. Capital spending far below depreciation → assets not being replaced (or wrong depreciation rates). Large spending should raise future operating cash
FinancingWhy money was raised: expansion, refinancing debt with equity, or survival (unsustainable)
DividendsPaying out most of the available cash leaves too little for investment or repaying debt
Cash flow ratiosCash ROCE = (cash generated from operations + interest and dividends received) ÷ capital employed. Cash from operations ÷ profit from operations (quality of earnings). Cash interest cover. Cash flow per share. Net debt ÷ cash from operations (years to repay). Capex ÷ depreciation.
Why cash mattersCash is harder to manipulate than profit; consistent gaps between profit and operating cash flow are a creative accounting red flag (Chapter 5). In Bangladesh, BSEC requires listed companies to report NOCFPS (net operating cash flow per share) and explain big differences from EPS.
4

What moves the ratios: economy, business and accounting choices

Economic eventsRecession, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, commodity prices, regulation. Segment data (IFRS 8) shows which parts are exposed. Inflation flatters historical-cost returns (old assets, current profits).
Business issuesType of business (retailers: low margin, high turnover; services: little PPE, high ROCE); quality of management; market conditions; actions (discounting cuts margin but raises turnover); changes (acquisitions near year end depress ROCE and distort working capital ratios: use IFRS 3 pro forma disclosures).
Context example: acquisition during the year

Revenue £10m (£8m); receivables £1.6m (£0.9m); a subsidiary bought in the year had receivables of £0.3m.

Raw growth in receivables: 1.6 ÷ 0.9+78%
Adjusted: 1.6 ÷ (0.9 + 0.3), compare revenue +25%+33%
Accounting choiceEffect on ratios
Revaluing PPEHigher equity and capital employed → lower ROCE and gearing; higher depreciation
Capitalising development costs, borrowing costsHigher profit and assets now; amortisation later
Depreciation method and lives; inventory cost formulaProfit and asset values
NCI measurement, goodwill, fair values on acquisitionGroup net assets, impairment exposure
Timing of transactions (delaying supplier payments, window dressing)Better year-end cash and liquidity

Ethical pressure: directors with bonuses, covenants or a sale in view may push accountants to choose favourable treatments or time transactions. The accountant must apply the Code (integrity, objectivity), challenge, escalate and, if needed, refuse or resign.

Industry analysis and non-financial measures

Context example: different industries, different ratios

Heavy manufacturing vs advertising and media.

RatioManufacturer / Media
ROCE13.9% / 36.6%
Net margin13.2% / 3.1%
Asset turnover1.05 / 11.8 times
Inventory turnover4.5 / 58.8 times
Gearing38.4% / 104.1%

The media company's main "asset", its people, is not on the balance sheet, so ROCE looks high. Compare only like with like, and use industry-specific measures: revenue per passenger mile (airlines), like-for-like sales and sales per square metre (retail), fees per partner and utilisation (professional firms), market share, customer numbers and churn, ARPU (telecoms).

Non-financial performance measures: customer satisfaction and complaints, quality and defect rates, delivery times, staff turnover and training, innovation (new product share), environmental and social measures (emissions, safety). They are often leading indicators but may be selective, unaudited and inconsistently defined.

6

Limitations of ratio analysis

Comparability

Different policies, estimates, year ends, sizes, business mixes and definitions of ratios.

Historical data

Past figures may not predict the future; inflation distorts historical cost.

Snapshot

Year-end balances may be untypical or window-dressed.

Missing items

Internally generated intangibles, staff skills, off-balance-sheet risks.

Manipulation

Creative accounting and one-off items.

No answers

Ratios raise questions; explanations need other information.

Exam focus

FSA questions give two years' (or two companies') data and a user. Calculate a focused set of ratios, adjust for distortions (acquisitions, one-offs, accounting errors you found earlier in the question), then explain why each moved, link to the business story, and conclude for the user (invest, lend, renegotiate). Always note limitations and further information needed. Marks come from interpretation, not arithmetic.
