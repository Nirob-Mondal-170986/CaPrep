1

Identifying a lease and the exemptions

Lease: a contract, or part of one, that conveys the right to use an identified asset for a period of time in exchange for consideration.
Three tests(1) Identified asset: specified explicitly or implicitly, and the supplier has no substantive right to substitute it (a swap only for repairs is not substantive). A physically distinct part (e.g. specific fibres in a cable) can qualify. (2) The customer gets substantially all the economic benefits from use. (3) The customer has the right to direct how and for what purpose it is used.
Exemptions (lessee may elect)Short-term: 12 months or less, no purchase option (by class of asset). Low value: low value when new (laptops, phones, small furniture), usable on its own and not highly dependent on other assets (lease by lease). Payments expensed straight-line. Separate non-lease components (maintenance) unless the practical expedient is used.
Worked examples: lease or not?

(1) Three specified tractors kept on the farm for 5 years; replaced only during repairs. (2) Combine harvesters "with VARIO cutters": supplier picks the machine and keeps them at its depot. (3) Any 50-seat coach from the supplier's fleet. (4) Ten specified council vehicles; council sets routes and use; replaced only for servicing. (5) Three specified dark fibres, lit and controlled by the customer for 10 years. (6) Oscar Co pays £32,000 for an 8-month machine lease.

(1) Identified; repair swaps not substantiveLease
(2) Supplier chooses the asset: not identifiedNot a lease
(3) Substantive substitution rightNot a lease
(4) Specified vehicles; council directs useLease
(5) Physically distinct capacity; customer directsLease
(6) Short-term exemption: expense 2/8 = £8,000 by 30 June; £24,000 prepaidExempt

Lessee accounting

Lease liabilityPV of payments not yet paid: fixed payments (less incentives receivable), variable payments based on an index or rate, expected residual value guarantee payments, purchase option if reasonably certain, termination penalties if expected. Discount at the rate implicit in the lease, or the lessee's incremental borrowing rate. Then add interest, deduct payments; remeasure for changes in term, index or assessment.
Right-of-use assetLease liability + payments made at or before commencement + initial direct costs + estimated dismantling costs − incentives received. Depreciate over the shorter of lease term and useful life (useful life if ownership transfers or a purchase option is reasonably certain). Impairment under IAS 36; revaluation model (IAS 16) or fair value (IAS 40) allowed.
Worked example: payments in arrears

Three annual payments of £10,000 from 31 Dec 20X1; implicit rate 10%.

Year: opening + interest − payment = closing
20X1: 24,869 + 2,487 − 10,00017,356
20X2: 17,356 + 1,736 − 10,0009,092
20X3: 9,092 + 909 − 10,000≈ 0
At end of 20X1: current = 17,356 − 9,092 = 8,264; non-current9,092
Worked example: payments in advance (IFRS 16 example 13)

5-year building lease; £50,000 at the start of each year; initial direct costs £20,000; incentive received £5,000; incremental borrowing rate 5%.

Lease liability: PV of the 4 future payments at 5%£177,297
Right-of-use asset: 177,297 + 50,000 + 20,000 − 5,000£242,297

Disclosure: depreciation by class, interest on lease liabilities, short-term and low-value expense, variable payments not in the liability, total cash outflow, additions, a maturity analysis of lease liabilities, and right-of-use assets by class (separately or within PPE with disclosure).

Sale and leaseback

First ask: is the transfer a sale under IFRS 15 (does the buyer obtain control: legal title, possession, risks and rewards, acceptance, no repurchase right)?

It is a sale(1) Right-of-use asset = carrying amount × (PV of lease payments ÷ fair value). (2) Total gain = fair value − carrying amount. (3) Recognise only the gain on rights transferred = total gain × (1 − PV ÷ FV). Lease liability = PV of payments.
It is not a saleKeep the asset and keep depreciating it; treat the proceeds as a financial liability under IFRS 9 at amortised cost. The buyer shows a financial asset, not the physical asset.

Off-market terms: proceeds above fair value: the excess is extra financing from the buyer (part of the liability, not the lease). Proceeds below fair value: treat the shortfall as a prepayment of rent. 2023 amendment (from 1 Jan 2024): where payments are variable and not based on an index, set the lease liability so no gain is recognised on the rights kept.

Worked examples

(1) Hill: carrying amount £5.04m, sold at fair value £7.92m, PV of leaseback payments £2.7528m (20 years, £240,000 in arrears, 6%). (2) Wigton: carrying amount £500,000, sold for £740,000 (fair value), PV £700,000. (3) Sydney: carrying amount £900,000; fair value £1m; sold for £1.2m; PV of 6 × £180,000 at 5% = £913,625. (4) Green: machine with carrying amount £720,000 sold for £480,000 but kept, used and insured by Green.

(1) Right-of-use: 5.04 × 2.7528/7.92£1,751,782
Gain: 2.88m × (1 − 2.7528/7.92)£1,878,982
Year 1: depreciation 1,751,782 ÷ 20 = 87,589; interest 2,752,800 × 6%£165,168
(2) Gain 240,000 − 240,000 × 700/740£12,973
(3) Lease liability 913,625 − 200,000 extra financing = 713,625; right-of-use 900,000 × 713,625/1m£642,262

Lessor accounting

Finance lease: transfers substantially all the risks and rewards of ownership. Otherwise it is an operating lease. Classify land and buildings separately.

Finance lease indicators: ownership transfers at the end; a bargain purchase option; lease term is the major part of economic life; PV of payments is substantially all of fair value; specialised asset only the lessee can use.

Operating leaseKeep and depreciate the asset; rental income straight-line; incentives reduce income over the term; initial direct costs added to the asset and spread. E.g. tow truck £75,000 over 10 years: depreciation £7,500, rent £10,000.
Finance leaseDerecognise the asset; recognise a receivable = net investment (PV of lease payments + unguaranteed residual value), plus initial direct costs. Finance income at a constant rate on the net investment.
Worked example: Leighton Machinery (finance lease, in advance)

Net investment £14,875 + arrangement fee £125; 8 annual rentals of £2,750 in advance; implicit rate 12.8%.

Receivable at start£15,000
Less first rental 2,750 = 12,250; interest 12.8% (income for 20X4)£1,568
Receivable at 31 Dec 20X4£13,818
Split: current £2,750; non-current (13,818 − 2,750)£11,068

Government grants (IAS 20) and borrowing costs (IAS 23)

IAS 20Recognise when there is reasonable assurance of meeting the conditions and receiving the grant. Income approach: match to the related costs (depreciable asset → over its life; land with conditions → over the period of the costs; costs already incurred → immediately). Asset grants: deferred income or deduct from the asset. Income grants: separate income or deduct from the expense. Repayment = change in estimate. Forgivable loans become grants when waiver is reasonably assured; below-market government loans: benefit treated as a grant.
IAS 23Capitalise borrowing costs directly attributable to a qualifying asset (takes a substantial time to get ready). Specific loans: actual cost less income from temporarily investing the funds. General loans: weighted average rate × spending, capped at actual costs. Start when spending, borrowing and activity begin; suspend during long interruptions; stop when substantially complete (by stage if parts are usable separately).
Interactive questions: Rechno and Zenzi

(1) Rechno borrows £15m at 10% on 1 Jan; Asset X gets £2.5m on 1 Jan and 1 July, Asset Y £5m on each date; spare funds earn 8%. (2) Zenzi's general loans: £120m at 10% and £80m at 9.5%; machine spending £30m on 1 Jan and £20m on 1 Oct.

(1) X: 5m × 10% − 2.5m × 8% × 6/12 = 500 − 100; cost £5.4m£400,000
Y: 10m × 10% − 5m × 8% × 6/12 = 1,000 − 200; cost £10.8m£800,000
(2) Rate (12 + 7.6) ÷ 200 = 9.8%: 30 × 9.8% + 20 × 9.8% × 3/12£3.43m

Capital vs income approach for grants: the capital view says grants are financing, not earned, so they go straight to equity. The income view (required) says they don't come from owners, are earned by meeting conditions, and are like negative taxes, so they belong in profit or loss.

Cash flow statements (IAS 7) and auditing leases

IAS 7 revision: operating (direct or indirect method), investing (buying and selling long-term assets and investments, loans made) and financing (shares and borrowings, and the principal part of lease payments). Non-cash transactions (debt converted to equity, new right-of-use assets) are excluded but disclosed. Lease interest may be operating or financing per policy; short-term and low-value lease payments are operating. Group cash flows are covered with consolidation.

Lease audit issueEvidence
Completeness and identificationRead contracts (and rental expense accounts) to find leases hidden as service contracts; test the substitution and control conditions; challenge use of the short-term or low-value exemption (a common way to keep debt off the balance sheet)
MeasurementAgree payments, term (including extension options likely to be used) and rate to the contract; recalculate liability and right-of-use asset, including initial costs and incentives; challenge the incremental borrowing rate and useful life
Sale and leasebackRead the agreements to decide whether control really passed; check fair value with a valuer; recompute the partial gain
LessorsAnalyse contract terms to decide finance vs operating; test residual values
DisclosureMaturity analysis, current and non-current split, IFRS 16 disclosures complete
Exam focus

IFRS 16 appears in most papers, often as a sale and leaseback or a disguised lease. Answer in order: is it a lease (or a sale)? → measure the liability and right-of-use asset → year-end figures (interest, depreciation, current/non-current split) → the correcting entry → audit risk (off-balance-sheet debt, covenant pressure) and procedures.
