P6

WACC

Typical: 6–8 marks = 12–16 minutes, plus 4–5 marks of theory. Tested in most years.

When to use: "Calculate the weighted average cost of capital" from market data on each source of finance.

  1. Cost of each source, after tax for debt: equity (dividend growth model or CAPM), preference shares, irredeemable debt, redeemable debt (IRR), bank loan.
  2. Market value of each source, ex-dividend and ex-interest. Bank loans at book value.
  3. Table: market value × cost. WACC = total of that column ÷ total market value.
Example data

10m ordinary shares, price Tk 32 cum-div. A dividend of Tk 2.00 is about to be paid. Four years ago the dividend was Tk 1.645. 2m 8% preference shares of Tk 10, price Tk 8. Tk 20m 9% irredeemable debentures, price Tk 90 per Tk 100. Tk 30m 10% bonds, price Tk 95 ex-interest, redeemable at par in 5 years. Bank loan Tk 10m at 12%. Tax 25%.

Traps
  • Cum-div / cum-interest prices: take off the dividend or interest that is about to be paid.
  • Growth: n years of growth = number of dividends minus one. Or use g = retention rate × return on equity.
  • Redeemable debt: tax relief is on the interest only, not on the redemption.
  • When WACC can be used: project has the same business risk, the same gearing, and is small relative to the company.
P7

Bond valuation and cost of redeemable / convertible debt

Typical: 5–7 marks = 10–14 minutes. Tested in 2022; feeds every WACC question.

When to use: "What is the market value of the bond?" (given the yield) or "What is the cost of debt?" (given the price).

  1. Price given the yield: PV of interest (annuity) + PV of redemption, at the investor's required return. No tax for the investor's view.
  2. Cost given the price: IRR of price now, after-tax interest, and redemption (as in P6).
  3. Convertible: compare redemption value with conversion value (shares × expected share price). Use the higher one.
Example data

(a) An 8% bond, redeemable at Tk 105 in 4 years. Investors require 10%. (b) A 10% convertible bond, price Tk 104. In 5 years the holder can take Tk 100 cash or 4 shares. Share price now Tk 22, growing at 6% a year. Tax 25%.

Traps
  • Two views: the investor's yield is before tax. The company's cost of debt is after tax.
  • Semi-annual interest: halve the coupon and the rate, and double the number of periods.
  • Price above par means the yield is below the coupon rate, and the other way round.
P8

Asset beta and equity beta – re-gearing

Typical: 6–8 marks = 12–16 minutes.

When to use: The project is in a different industry (different business risk) or will be financed with different gearing. The company's own WACC cannot be used.

  1. Find a proxy company in the new industry. Take its equity beta.
  2. Degear: βa = βe ÷ [1 + D(1 − T) ÷ E], using the proxy's gearing.
  3. Strip out other activities if the proxy has more than one business (asset beta is a weighted average).
  4. Regear: βe = βa × [1 + D(1 − T) ÷ E], using the project's (or your own) gearing.
  5. CAPM for the cost of equity, then the project WACC.
Example data

A food company plans an electronics project, financed 40% debt and 60% equity. Proxy company: equity beta 1.5, gearing 20% debt to 80% equity. The proxy is 75% electronics and 25% retail; retail has an asset beta of 0.9. Risk-free rate 6%, market return 11%, tax 25%. Project debt costs 8% before tax.

Particulars Workings Amount
Proxy asset beta (degear) 1.5 ÷ [1 + 20 × 0.75 ÷ 80] 1.263
Electronics asset beta (strip out retail) (1.263 − 25% × 0.9) ÷ 75% 1.384
Project equity beta (regear) 1.384 × [1 + 40 × 0.75 ÷ 60] 2.076
Cost of equity (CAPM) 6% + 2.076 × (11% − 6%) 16.38%
After-tax cost of debt 8% × (1 − 0.25) 6.00%
Project discount rate (WACC) 60% × 16.38% + 40% × 6.00% 12.23%
Traps
  • Degear with the proxy's gearing. Regear with your own. Mixing them up is the most common error.
  • Market values of debt and equity, not book values.
  • Debt beta is taken as zero unless the question gives one.
  • Several proxies: degear each one, then average the asset betas.
P9

CAPM, alpha and portfolio beta

Typical: 5–10 marks = 10–20 minutes. 50 marks across the past papers.

When to use: "What return is required?", "Is the share over- or under-valued?", or "What is the beta / risk of the portfolio?"

  1. Required return = Rf + β(Rm − Rf).
  2. Alpha = expected return − required return. Positive = under-valued (buy). Negative = over-valued (sell).
  3. Portfolio beta = weighted average of the betas, using market values as weights.
  4. Beta from data: β = covariance with the market ÷ market variance = correlation × σ(share) ÷ σ(market).
  5. Two-share portfolio risk: σp = √[w₁²σ₁² + w₂²σ₂² + 2w₁w₂ρσ₁σ₂].
Example data

Risk-free rate 6%, market return 12%. Portfolio: A 50%, B 30%, C 20% by market value.

Share Weight Beta Required return Expected return Alpha View
A 50% 1.4 6% + 1.4 × 6% = 14.4% 16.0% +1.6% Under-valued: buy
B 30% 0.8 6% + 0.8 × 6% = 10.8% 10.0% −0.8% Over-valued: sell
C 20% 1.1 6% + 1.1 × 6% = 12.6% 12.6% Nil Fairly priced
Portfolio 100% 1.16 12.96% 13.52% +0.56%

Portfolio beta = 50% × 1.4 + 30% × 0.8 + 20% × 1.1 = 1.16.

Traps
  • Market premium: if the question gives "market risk premium 6%", that is already (Rm − Rf). Do not deduct Rf again.
  • Diversification removes unsystematic risk only. CAPM rewards systematic risk (beta) only.
  • Weighted average works for beta, not for standard deviation, unless correlation is +1.
P10

TERP and the value of a right

Typical: 3–9 marks = 6–18 minutes. Tested in 2024 and Nov-Dec 2025.

When to use: A rights issue: "calculate the theoretical ex-rights price" and "show the effect on a shareholder's wealth".

  1. TERP = (N × cum-rights price + issue price) ÷ (N + 1), for a 1-for-N issue.
  2. Value of a right = TERP − issue price (per new share). Divide by N for the value per existing share.
  3. Shareholder wealth: compare wealth before and after for: take up, sell, part of each, do nothing.
  4. If the money earns an NPV: new price = (old market value + cash raised + NPV) ÷ total shares.
Example data

2m shares, price Tk 45. Rights issue of 1 for 5 at Tk 36. A shareholder owns 1,000 shares. Second case: the cash is used for a project with an NPV of Tk 6m.

Traps
  • Value of a right: say whether it is per new share or per existing share.
  • Issue costs: deduct them from the cash raised.
  • Wealth does not change if the holder takes up or sells. Only "do nothing" loses.
  • Rights given as a discount: "20% discount" means issue price = 80% of the current price.
P11

EPS and gearing under financing options

Typical: 8 marks + 5 marks advice = 26 minutes. Tested in Nov-Dec 2025 Q1.

When to use: "Should the company raise the money by a rights issue or by debt?" with forecast EPS and gearing.

  1. Columns: current, option 1 (equity), option 2 (debt).
  2. Profit down to EPS: PBIT, interest, tax, earnings, number of shares.
  3. Gearing = debt ÷ (debt + equity), and interest cover = PBIT ÷ interest.
  4. Break-even PBIT: set the two EPS formulas equal and solve.
  5. Advice: higher EPS v higher financial risk, control, covenants, security, market reaction.
Example data

PBIT Tk 500m now, Tk 620m after the investment. Existing 10% debt Tk 400m. Tax 25%. 100m shares, book equity Tk 1,200m. Tk 300m is needed: either a 1-for-5 rights issue at Tk 15, or a 12% bond.

Rights issue: 100m ÷ 5 = 20m new shares × Tk 15 = Tk 300m. Bond interest: 300 × 12% = 36, so total interest 76. Retained profit for the year is ignored in equity.

Traps
  • Gearing definition: use the one in the question (debt ÷ equity or debt ÷ total capital). State it.
  • Rights issue shares: cash needed ÷ issue price. Do not use the market price.
  • Advice marks: do not stop at EPS. Mention risk, interest cover, covenants and control.
P12

Dividend valuation model and residual dividend policy

Typical: 4–10 marks = 8–20 minutes. 53 marks across the past papers.

When to use: "Estimate the share price", "what growth rate is implied?", or "what dividend follows from a residual policy?"

  1. Constant growth: P0 = D0(1 + g) ÷ (ke − g). P0 is the ex-div price.
  2. Growth: g = retention rate × return on equity, or from past dividends.
  3. Two-stage growth: PV of each dividend in the high-growth years, plus PV of the terminal value.
  4. Residual policy: dividend = earnings − equity share of the capital budget.
Example data

(a) EPS Tk 10, payout 40%, return on equity 15%, cost of equity 14%. (b) Same dividend, but growth is 15% for 3 years and 5% after that. (c) Earnings Tk 200m, capital budget Tk 150m, target structure 60% equity, 20m shares.

Traps
  • D0 or D1: "just paid" = D0, so multiply by (1 + g). "Expected next year" = D1, use it directly.
  • Terminal value sits at the end of the high-growth period. Discount it with that year's factor.
  • Retaining at a return below ke destroys value. Tested in the 2026 mock (Jack Mill).
  • Theory link: Modigliani and Miller say dividend policy is irrelevant in a perfect market; in practice signalling, clientele and tax matter.
