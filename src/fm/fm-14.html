P13

Cash conversion cycle (cash operating cycle)

Typical: 5–9 marks = 10–18 minutes. Tested in Mar-Apr 2024.

When to use: "Calculate the cash operating cycle" or "comment on working capital management".

  1. Inventory days: raw materials ÷ purchases; work in progress and finished goods ÷ cost of sales; all × 365.
  2. Receivable days = receivables ÷ credit sales × 365.
  3. Payable days = payables ÷ credit purchases × 365.
  4. Cycle = inventory days + receivable days − payable days.
  5. Comment: compare with last year or the industry, and put a Taka value on any change.
Example data

Tk '000: credit sales 36,500; cost of sales 25,550; purchases 21,900. Raw materials 1,800; work in progress 700; finished goods 2,100; receivables 4,500; payables 2,400. Overdraft rate 12%.

Particulars Workings Amount (days)
Raw material days 1,800 ÷ 21,900 × 365 30
Work in progress days 700 ÷ 25,550 × 365 10
Finished goods days 2,100 ÷ 25,550 × 365 30
Receivable days 4,500 ÷ 36,500 × 365 45
Payable days 2,400 ÷ 21,900 × 365 (40)
Cash conversion cycle 30 + 10 + 30 + 45 − 40 75 days

Value of a change: receivable days cut from 45 to 35

Particulars Workings Amount (Tk '000)
Fall in receivables 36,500 × 10 ÷ 365 1,000
Interest saved each year 1,000 × 12% 120
Traps
  • Right base: receivables on sales, payables and raw materials on purchases, the rest on cost of sales. If purchases are not given, use cost of sales and say so.
  • Year-end v average balances: use the average if both years are given.
  • A shorter cycle is not always better: stock-outs, lost customers and unhappy suppliers are the costs.
  • Overtrading signs: fast sales growth, rising payable days, falling liquidity.
P14

Factoring – cost comparison

Typical: 6–10 marks = 12–20 minutes. Tested in Jul-Aug 2024 and the 2026 mock.

When to use: "Should the company use a factor?" or "which factor: with recourse or non-recourse?"

  1. Columns: current position and each factor offer.
  2. Rows: bad debts, administration cost, finance cost of receivables, factor fee.
  3. Finance cost = receivables × rate. With a factor: advance % at the factor's rate, the rest at the overdraft rate.
  4. Compare total annual cost. Lowest wins. Then give non-financial points.
Example data

Credit sales Tk 73,000k a year. Receivable days 60. Bad debts 2% of sales. Credit control costs Tk 400k a year. Overdraft 13%. Factor A (non-recourse): days fall to 40, fee 2.5% of sales, advances 80% at 15%. Factor B (with recourse): days fall to 45, fee 1% of sales, advances 80% at 14%. Both factors take over credit control.

Tk '000 Current Factor A (non-recourse) Factor B (recourse)
Bad debts (2% × 73,000) 1,460 – 1,460
Credit control cost 400 – –
Finance cost of receivables 1,560 1,168 1,242
Factor fee – 1,825 730
Total annual cost 3,420 2,993 3,432
Saving v current 427 (12)

Receivables: current 73,000 × 60 ÷ 365 = 12,000; A = 8,000; B = 9,000. Finance cost: current 12,000 × 13%. A: 8,000 × (80% × 15% + 20% × 13%). B: 9,000 × (80% × 14% + 20% × 13%).

Decision: choose Factor A, the lowest cost. Also consider: customer relations, loss of control, and how hard it is to stop using a factor.

Traps
  • Non-recourse means the factor bears the bad debts. With recourse, bad debts stay with the company.
  • Fee base: the fee is on sales (turnover), not on receivables.
  • Advance interest applies only for the time the money is outstanding (the new receivable days).
  • If sales change because of the policy, add the extra contribution as a benefit.
P15

Early settlement discount

Typical: 4–9 marks = 8–18 minutes. Tested in Jul-Aug 2022.

When to use: "Should we offer a cash discount to customers?" or "should we take the supplier's discount?"

  1. Annual cost of a discount = [100 ÷ (100 − d)]^(365 ÷ days saved) − 1. Compare with the overdraft rate.
  2. Offering to customers: cost = discount % × sales that take it. Benefit = fall in receivables × finance rate, plus lower bad debts.
  3. Decision: offer only if benefit is above cost. Take a supplier's discount if its annual rate is above your borrowing rate.
Example data

(a) A supplier offers 2% for payment in 10 days instead of 45. (b) The company sells Tk 36,500k on 45 days' credit. It plans to offer 2% for payment in 10 days. 40% of customers (by value) will take it. Bad debts will fall from 1% to 0.8% of sales. Overdraft rate 12%.

(a) Supplier's discount

Particulars Workings Amount
Days saved 45 − 10 35
Annual cost of not taking it (100 ÷ 98)^(365 ÷ 35) − 1 23.5%

Decision: 23.5% is above the 12% overdraft rate, so take the discount and pay on day 10.

(b) Offering a discount to customers

Particulars Workings Amount (Tk '000)
Receivables now 36,500 × 45 ÷ 365 4,500
Receivables after: no discount 60% × 36,500 × 45 ÷ 365 2,700
Receivables after: discount takers 40% × 36,500 × 98% × 10 ÷ 365 392
Fall in receivables 4,500 − 3,092 1,408
Finance cost saved 1,408 × 12% 169
Bad debts saved 36,500 × (1% − 0.8%) 73
Cost of discount 2% × 40% × 36,500 (292)
Net benefit / (cost) (50)

Decision: do not offer the discount. It costs more than it saves.

Traps
  • Simple rate version: d ÷ (100 − d) × 365 ÷ days saved. Fine if the question does not ask for the compound rate.
  • Discount takers pay 98%, so their receivable is on the net amount.
  • Do not forget bad debt and administration savings if the question gives them.
P16

Forecast income statement and balance sheet

Typical: 14 marks + 4 marks comment = 36 minutes. Tested in Nov-Dec 2023 and Nov-Dec 2025.

When to use: "Prepare a forecast income statement and statement of financial position" from growth rates and working capital ratios.

  1. Income statement down to retained profit. Use the stated growth, margins and rates.
  2. Non-current assets = opening + additions − depreciation.
  3. Working capital from the day ratios: inventory and payables on cost of sales, receivables on revenue.
  4. Equity = opening + retained profit. Loans = opening − repayments + new.
  5. Cash is the balancing figure. A negative figure is an overdraft.
  6. Comment: liquidity (current and quick ratio), gearing, and the cash need.
Example data

Opening balance sheet (Tk m): non-current assets 1,000; inventory 150; receivables 200; cash 80; payables 90; 10% loan 400; equity 940. Revenue was 1,500. Forecast: revenue +20%, gross margin 40%, operating expenses 15% of revenue, depreciation 10% of opening assets plus additions, capital spending 150, interest on the opening loan, loan repayment 50 at year end, tax 25% paid in the year, dividend 40% of profit after tax. Inventory 45 days and payables 30 days of cost of sales; receivables 60 days.

Comment: cash falls from 80 to 47.5 because growth ties up cash in receivables. Current ratio = 5.4. Gearing (loan ÷ equity) falls to 33%.

Traps
  • Cash is last. Never guess it. If it comes out negative, call it an overdraft and say financing is needed.
  • Interest base: opening loan unless told otherwise.
  • Tax unpaid at year end is a current liability, not a cash flow.
  • The cash proof is optional. Do it only if time allows; it catches errors.
