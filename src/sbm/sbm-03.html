1

Acquisitions and strategic alliances

Why acquireSpeed to enter a market; get capabilities, brands, customers; economies of scale and synergy; remove a rival; diversify; use spare cash or a higher share price; defensive reasons.
Better-off testsIs the acquirer better off than before, than organic growth, and than alternative uses of the money? Also: is the price below the real value, and will the acquired business be better off as part of the group (the "ownership" test)?
Way to payEffect
Cash (from reserves or loans)Target shareholders get certainty; acquirer's gearing and interest rise; no dilution of control
Share exchangeNo cash needed; target holders share future gains and risk; dilutes existing owners; EPS may fall or rise depending on the relative P/E ratios
Mixed or convertiblesBalance cash and dilution
Acquisitions and EPS (rule of thumb)

If the acquirer buys with shares, its EPS rises when the acquirer's P/E ratio is higher than the target's, and falls when lower (ignoring synergies).

Example: acquirer EPS 10, share price 150 (P/E 15). Target EPS 5, offer price 60 (P/E 12). Acquirer issues 60 ÷ 150 = 0.4 new shares per target share. The target adds 5 of earnings per target share for 0.4 new acquirer shares = 12.5 earnings per new share, above 10. So EPS rises.

With debt finance, compare the after-tax interest with the earnings acquired. Always check the effect on gearing, covenants and cash flow, and on value, not just EPS.

Issues to consider: price and valuation (Chapter 12); due diligence (Chapter 2); integration plan; culture; key staff retention; regulation and competition law; financing; goodwill and impairment; hidden liabilities; assurance procedures on the target's figures. Strategic alliances: choose partners with compatible aims, complementary skills and trust; agree governance, contributions, profit sharing and exit terms.

Aligning organisational structure and strategy

Structure follows strategy (Chandler): the structure must support the chosen strategy, the size and the environment.

Choosing: think of strategy, size, technology, environment (stable or turbulent), culture, geography and people skills. Configuration includes how centralised decisions are and how wide spans of control are.

3

Managing change

Change is needed because of external shifts, new strategy, technology, poor performance or new leadership. People often resist change (fear, loss of status, habit, poor communication), so a method matters.

Lewin's three-stage model

Unfreeze → change → refreeze.

Unfreeze: show why the present way no longer works. Change (move): train, communicate, involve people, try new ways. Refreeze: embed with rewards, systems, procedures and culture so the change lasts.

Lewin's force-field analysis

List driving forces and restraining forces.

Change happens when driving forces exceed restraining forces. Often it is more effective to reduce the restraining forces (fears, lack of skills) than to push harder.

Kotter's eight stepsMeaning
1 Create urgencyShow the threat or opportunity
2 Build a guiding coalitionInfluential leaders who support change
3 Form a vision and strategyClear picture of the future
4 Communicate the visionOften and in many ways
5 Empower actionRemove barriers; give authority and skills
6 Create short-term winsVisible early success
7 Consolidate gainsBuild on wins; more change
8 Anchor in cultureMake new ways normal

Types of change (by speed and scope): evolution (slow, gradual), adaptation (incremental, fits culture), revolution (fast, transforming), reconstruction (fast, within existing culture, as in turnaround). A turnaround restores a failing firm: stabilise cash, cut costs, replace management, refocus. Digital transformation needs leadership, skills, data, process redesign and culture, not just technology.

4

Cost reduction, supply chain, BPR and outsourcing

Cost reduction techniquesValue engineering and value analysis; target costing; lean and waste removal; process automation; economies of scale; renegotiating with suppliers; reducing complexity. Risk: cutting quality, morale or capability that supports the strategy (cost cutting must be sustainable).
Supply chain managementPlan, source, make, deliver and return. Drivers: facilities, inventory, transport, information, sourcing, pricing. Information sharing and supplier selection (quality, cost, delivery, reliability, financial strength, sustainability) lower cost and risk. Strategic procurement and e-procurement cut price, time and error.
Business process re-engineering (BPR)Points
IdeaRadical redesign of processes (not departments) to achieve big gains in cost, speed, quality (Hammer and Champy)
PrinciplesOrganise around outcomes; remove steps that add no value; use IT to do tasks once, at source; link parallel activities
ProblemsDisruption, staff resistance, job losses, high cost, often fails without top-level support; control and audit trails must be preserved
OutsourcingBenefitsRisks
Hand a non-core activity to a specialist (including IT/IS)Lower cost, specialist skill, flexibility, focus on core competencesLoss of control and know-how, quality, hidden costs, lock-in, data security, reputation, dependency

Successful outsourcing: outsource only non-core activities (value chain and core competence), choose the provider carefully, write a clear service level agreement, manage the relationship and monitor performance. For offshoring (for example to India or Eastern Europe) consider language, time zone, law, political and currency risk. Use assurance reports on the provider's controls. Cyber security in the supply chain: screen suppliers, limit data access, test controls, train people, plan for breaches.

5

Operations strategy and management

Operations turn inputs (materials, information, customers, labour, capital) into outputs through a transformation process. Operations strategy must support competitive advantage.

Performance objectives

Quality, speed, dependability, flexibility, cost.

The four Vs

Volume, variety, variation in demand, visibility to the customer. They shape the right design.

Capacity planning

Match capacity to demand: level, chase or demand-management policy; cost of idle capacity against lost sales.

JIT (just in time)

Produce or buy only when needed: low inventory, high quality, close suppliers. Risk: disruption stops production.

Cost of qualityExamples
PreventionTraining, design, supplier selection
AppraisalInspection, testing, audits
Internal failureScrap, rework, downtime
External failureReturns, warranty claims, lost customers and reputation

Total quality management (TQM): a culture of continuous improvement where everyone owns quality, "get it right first time", focus on the customer, measure and use data. Spending more on prevention usually lowers total quality cost.

6

Evaluating functional strategies

Functional strategies (marketing, operations, HR, IS/IT, finance) must support the corporate and business strategy and fit each other. To evaluate one, ask:

  • Does it support the chosen competitive strategy (for example, low-cost operations for a cost leader)?
  • Is it consistent with the other functions (marketing promises that operations cannot deliver)?
  • Are resources and skills available, and what are the costs and benefits?
  • Are risks, timing and measures defined?
7

Business plans

Contents of a business planExecutive summary; business description and objectives; market analysis and customers; competition; products and strategy; operations and people; marketing plan; financial forecasts (income, cash flow, balance sheet, break-even); funding required and use; risks and assumptions; timeline.
Critiquing a planAre the assumptions realistic and supported? Is the market evidence sound? Is cash flow sufficient, with sensitivity analysis? Is the team credible? Are risks and exit routes covered? Consistent with strategy and objectives?

Assurance over prospective financial information (PFI): an accountant can examine forecasts and projections. The conclusion is limited to whether the assumptions provide a reasonable basis, whether the PFI is properly prepared on those assumptions, and whether it is presented consistently with the accounting policies. Before accepting, consider the intended use, who relies on it, the competence of the team and the quality of management's process. Assurance does not guarantee the results will be achieved.

Exam focus

Likely requirements: advise on how to implement a strategy (structure, change, outsourcing), evaluate an acquisition (better-off, financing, EPS), recommend a change plan with resistance management, or critique a business plan and forecasts.
