Valuation methods: choosing the model

There is no single correct value. Value depends on purpose (takeover, listing, tax, dispute), perspective (buyer or seller) and information. Reported accounting profit can be distorted by accounting policies, one-off items, smoothing and unrecorded assets; adjust it first. Use several methods and compare.

Last dividend 20; growth 4%; cost of equity 12%.

P₀ = 20 × 1.04 ÷ (0.12 − 0.04) = 20.8 ÷ 0.08 = 260 per share. Growth can be estimated as retention rate × return on equity: g = b × r.

P/E model

Earnings 5 million; similar listed company P/E 12.

Value = 5m × 12 = 60 million. For an unquoted company apply a discount (see section 3).

Free cash flow, SVA, EVA and MVA

Free cash flow (to the firm) = operating profit after tax (NOPAT) + depreciation − capital expenditure − increase in working capital. Terminal value (Gordon) = FCF in the last year × (1 + g) ÷ (WACC − g).
DCF example

Free cash flows: year 1 = 100, year 2 = 110, year 3 = 120; then grow at 3% a year for ever. WACC 10%.

Year 1: 100 ÷ 1.1090.9
Year 2: 110 ÷ 1.10²90.9
Year 3: 120 ÷ 1.10³90.2
Terminal value at year 3: 120 × 1.03 ÷ (0.10 − 0.03) = 1,765.7; PV ÷ 1.10³1,326.6
Value of the business (enterprise value)1,598.6

Equity value = enterprise value − net debt (add cash, deduct debt and other claims such as pension deficit). Terminal value is often 60–80% of the total, so test with a range of growth and WACC.

Shareholder value analysis (SVA, Rappaport)Value = PV of free cash flows over a forecast period (from value drivers: sales growth, operating margin, tax rate, incremental investment in fixed capital and working capital) + residual value, at WACC. Shows which drivers create value. Value is created only if growth earns more than the cost of capital.
EVA and MVAEVA = NOPAT − (capital employed × WACC): a year's surplus over the cost of capital. Example: NOPAT 150, capital 1,000, WACC 10% → EVA 50. MVA = market value of the firm − capital invested (the present value of all future EVAs). Positive EVA creates value. Adjust accounting figures (R&D, leases) for a fair view.

Valuing an acquisition target

ApproachUse in a takeover
Asset-basedFloor value; useful for asset-rich targets and break-up
P/EApply the acquirer's or the sector's P/E to the target's earnings (adjusted)
Free cash flowDCF of the target's cash flows (with synergy effects), compared with the offer price: NPV of acquisition = value − price
EVA approachValue = capital + PV of future EVAs
Adjusted present value (APV)Base-case NPV as if all-equity financed (discount at the ungeared cost of equity) + PV of financing side effects (tax shield on debt interest, issue costs, subsidised loans)
APV: tax shield example

Debt of 1,000 at 6% for 5 years raised to fund the deal; tax rate 25%.

Annual tax shield = 1,000 × 6% × 25% = 15. PV at the cost of debt 6% over 5 years = 15 × 4.212 = 63.2. Add this to the base-case NPV and deduct issue costs. APV suits projects whose gearing will change.

SynergyExtra value from combining: revenue (cross-selling), cost (scale, overhead), financial (lower borrowing cost, tax losses). Value of the target to the acquirer = standalone value + PV of synergies. Do not pay all the synergy to the seller; check synergies are real, achievable and net of integration cost.
Other pointsControl premium (usually 20–40% above the market price); price must not exceed value to the acquirer; check pensions deficits and accounting policy differences; due diligence (Chapter 2); IFRS 3 accounting: goodwill, intangible assets, fair values, costs expensed.
4

Unquoted companies, start-ups and loss-making businesses

  • Unquoted companies: no market price. Use a P/E from a similar quoted firm and apply a discount for lack of marketability (often 20–40%) and for smaller size and higher risk. Check earnings are normalised (directors' pay, related-party items).
  • Majority holding (controls the dividend policy, board and strategy): value on the earnings or cash-flow of the whole business. Minority holding (no control): value on dividends received; usually a lower value per share (minority discount).
  • Start-ups: little history. Use DCF with scenarios, a venture capital method (target exit value ÷ required multiple of return), comparable transactions, milestones. High uncertainty; test assumptions; consider real options.
  • Loss-making businesses: value on assets (net realisable), or on forecast turnaround earnings, or the value of its customers, licences or technology to a buyer.
  • Bargain purchase: price below fair value of net assets gives a gain in profit or loss under IFRS 3, but check that all assets and liabilities have been identified.

Valuation of debt

The value of debt is the present value of future interest and redemption at the investor's required yield (the market yield).

Debt typeValue
IrredeemableValue = annual interest ÷ required yield
RedeemablePV of interest (annuity) + PV of redemption value

International Valuation Standards (IVS)

The IVS, issued by the International Valuation Standards Council, set principles for valuations used in financial reporting, lending, tax and transactions. They have general standards (scope of work, investigations, reporting, bases of value such as market value, assumptions) and asset standards (for example business, intangible assets, real property, financial instruments).

  • Basis of value: state clearly (market value, fair value under IFRS 13, investment value, liquidation value).
  • Valuer competence and objectivity: disclose conflicts and the use of experts.
  • Documentation: record assumptions, data, methods and sensitivity. Valuations for financial statements must meet IFRS 13 (hierarchy of inputs).
Exam focus

Typical tasks: value a target by two or three methods and compare to the offer price; discuss limitations of each; calculate EVA or APV; value debt or a convertible; treat an unquoted company (discount); comment on synergy. Always state assumptions and a recommended range, not just one figure.
