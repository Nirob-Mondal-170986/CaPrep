Strategic Choice

1

Generating options: generic strategies

Porter's generic strategyHow it worksNeedsRisks
Cost leadershipLowest cost in the industry, sell at average or lower priceEconomies of scale, tight control, efficient process, cheap inputsRivals copy or cut costs further; quality neglected; technology change
DifferentiationUnique features, brand or service; premium priceInnovation, brand, quality, customer insightPremium too high; imitation; customers stop valuing the difference
Focus (niche)Serve a narrow segment by cost focus or differentiation focusDeep knowledge of the segmentSegment too small; larger rivals enter; tastes change

Stuck in the middle: a firm that pursues no generic strategy clearly gets low profit. Limitations of Porter's model: some firms succeed with both low cost and differentiation (mass customisation); markets change; it ignores resources and capabilities; hard to classify real firms.

Assurance angle: third-party assurance reports (for example on quality or sustainability claims) can support a differentiation strategy by making claims credible.

3

Choosing between options: suitability, acceptability, feasibility

Suitability

Does it fit the objectives, the environment and the firm's capabilities? Does it exploit strengths and meet customer needs?

Acceptability

Will stakeholders accept the return, the risk and the impact (shareholders, lenders, staff, regulators, community)? Test with ratios, shareholder value, scenarios.

Feasibility

Can it be done? Money, people, skills, time, technology, suppliers, legal permission.

ESG

Does it meet environmental, social and governance expectations, and will it still be acceptable in future?

Exam method

For each option write a short S, A and F assessment using facts from the scenario, then compare the options (a table helps) and give a recommendation with conditions. State what further information you would need.

4

Evaluating options: investment appraisal, risk, due diligence

Investment decisions: use appraisal (NPV, IRR, payback: Chapter 17), consider strategic value (options created), compare with alternatives, and test risk by sensitivity, scenario and simulation. Risk analysis: identify what could go wrong, its likelihood and impact, and how to reduce it.

Due diligenceWhat it checks
FinancialQuality of earnings and cash flow, assets and liabilities, debt, working capital, forecasts, accounting policies
CommercialMarket, customers, competitors, contracts, pricing, growth assumptions
OperationalProcesses, capacity, suppliers, quality, people, cost base
TechnicalTechnology, plant, intellectual property, product readiness
IT and cyber securitySystems, data protection, breaches, resilience, licences
LegalOwnership, contracts, litigation, licences, regulation, employment
TaxTax exposures, compliance, structure, tax attributes

Professional scepticism is a questioning mind and critical assessment of evidence: do not accept management's forecasts at face value; look for bias, inconsistency and missing information; corroborate claims. It is vital in due diligence, forecasts and valuations.

Big data and business modelsData can be an asset: it improves decisions, personalises products, predicts demand and creates new revenue. Competitive advantage needs good data, skilled analysis and permission to use it (privacy). Risks: poor quality, bias, security, legal limits.
Questions to ask about data strategyWhat data do we hold? Is it accurate and legal to use? Which decisions would it improve? Who owns the analysis? What does it cost to protect?

International value chain: decide which activities to place where (cheap labour, skills, nearness to market, tax). Globalisation raises competition but opens markets; multinationals face tax rules (transfer pricing, tax havens) and reputation risk.

Foreign exchange in the accounts (IAS 21) and in the business

Exchange rates affect both the financial statements and commercial results.

  • Transaction: record at the spot rate on the date; at the year end retranslate monetary items at the closing rate (difference in profit or loss); non-monetary items at cost stay at the historical rate.
  • Foreign operation: translate assets and liabilities at the closing rate, income and expenses at the average rate; difference to OCI (translation reserve).
  • Commercial effect: a stronger home currency makes exports dearer and imports cheaper; a weaker one does the opposite. Hedging (Chapter 15) can protect.
6

Digital strategies

A digital strategy uses digital technology to change how the business creates value, serves customers and operates. It is part of corporate strategy, not only an IT plan.

ElementsCustomer experience and channels; data and analytics; automation (RPA) of routine work; AI and machine learning; cloud services; platforms and ecosystems; cyber security; digital skills and culture.
Digital disruptionShift from products to experiences and from ownership to access (subscription, sharing); platform-based models connect buyers and sellers and grow through network effects. Incumbents must respond or be displaced.
Step in developing a digital strategyWhat to do
1 Vision and objectivesLink to corporate strategy and customer needs
2 Assess current positionDigital maturity, systems, data, skills, competitors
3 Choose initiativesPrioritise by value, cost, risk, feasibility
4 Plan the changeRoadmap, budget, governance, people
5 Implement and measurePilot, scale, KPIs, benefits tracking

Cloud accounting (software as a service): real-time data, lower IT cost, remote access, automatic updates; risks: data security, dependence on the provider, internet failure, regulation on data location. Software selection: define requirements, long-list, demonstrations, evaluate fit, cost, support and security, pilot, contract and implementation plan.

Exam focus

Expect a scenario where you must choose and justify a growth method or entry mode, assess an acquisition or international move, list due diligence areas, or advise on digital change. Use S-A-F, state risks, and recommend.
