Strategic Analysis

What strategy is, how organisations set objectives, and the tools used to analyse the external environment, internal capability and current position: PESTEL, Porter's five forces, resource-based analysis, the product life cycle, BCG, the value chain, gap analysis, SWOT, plus technology developments and ESG.

How the exam uses this chapter

The question will not name the model. It gives a scenario and a requirement such as "evaluate the company's position". You must choose the right tool (for example Porter's five forces for an industry problem, BCG for a product portfolio, resource analysis for a capability problem) and apply it to the facts. Most of this is revision from Professional Level; the Advanced Level skill is choosing and applying.

1

Strategic management

Strategy: the direction and scope of an organisation over the long term, which achieves advantage in a changing environment through its configuration of resources, to meet the needs of markets and fulfil stakeholder expectations (Johnson, Scholes and Whittington).
Characteristics of strategic decisionsLong-term and wide in scope; relate to the fit between the organisation and its environment; need big resource commitments; hard to reverse; uncertain; shaped by values and stakeholder expectations; involve change.
Elements of strategic management1 Strategic analysis (position: environment, capability, expectations) → 2 Strategic choice (options, evaluation, selection) → 3 Strategic implementation (structure, resources, change, control) → 4 Review and control (performance, feedback).
ApproachIdeaStrengthWeakness
Prescriptive (rational planning)Analyse, choose, implement in a planned sequence from the top (Ansoff)Structured, clear, good for stable conditionsSlow, assumes good forecasts, ignores learning and politics
Emergent (Mintzberg)Strategy develops from experience, experiments and day-to-day decisions; "crafting" strategyFlexible, uses front-line knowledge, fits turbulent marketsLack of direction; hard to control and communicate
2

Goals, objectives and stakeholders

Mission says why the organisation exists; vision says what it wants to become; values guide behaviour; goals are the general aims; objectives are specific and measurable (SMART); targets are the numbers that track them. Objectives form a hierarchy: mission → corporate objectives → unit objectives → operational targets. Management by objectives (MBO) agrees objectives down the line.

TypeExamples
Primary (financial)Return on capital employed, earnings per share growth, dividend growth, share price and total shareholder return, profit margin, cash generation
Secondary (support primary)Market share, product quality, customer satisfaction, innovation, staff turnover, environmental targets
Business strategy and financial strategyThe business strategy (which markets, products, capabilities) must be financed. Financial strategy covers the investment decision, the financing decision (debt or equity, gearing) and the dividend decision. A good strategy needs both to fit: for example high growth needs equity or retained profit; a mature cash cow can carry more debt and pay higher dividends.
Value-based management (VBM)Run the business to create shareholder value: invest only where the return exceeds the cost of capital. Measures: total shareholder return, economic value added (EVA = NOPAT − capital × WACC), market value added. Implication: link strategy, planning and pay to value drivers. Risk: short-termism and neglect of other stakeholders.
Stakeholder management: Mendelow's power/interest grid

Plot each stakeholder by power and interest to decide how to manage them.

Low interestHigh interest
High powerKeep satisfied (regulators, large lenders)Key players: manage closely (major shareholders, key customers)
Low powerMinimal effort (general public)Keep informed (employees, local community)

Interests can conflict (shareholders want dividends, employees want pay, regulators want compliance). Strategy must be acceptable to the stakeholders who matter most.

3

The external environment: PESTEL, five forces, competitors

Political

Government stability, policy, trade rules, regulation.

Economic

Growth, inflation, interest and exchange rates, income.

Social

Demographics, culture, attitudes, lifestyle.

Technological

Automation, digital channels, R&D, obsolescence.

Environmental

Climate, resources, waste, sustainability rules.

Legal

Employment, data protection, competition, safety law.

PESTEL lists the macro factors. Always say which are most important for this organisation and whether each is an opportunity or a threat.

Porter's five forcesThe force is strong when
Threat of new entrantsLow entry barriers (little capital, no economies of scale, easy distribution, weak brands)
Threat of substitutesClose alternatives at similar or lower price, low switching cost
Bargaining power of buyersFew large buyers, undifferentiated product, low switching cost, price-sensitive
Bargaining power of suppliersFew suppliers, unique inputs, high switching cost, threat of forward integration
Rivalry among competitorsMany equal rivals, slow growth, high fixed costs, high exit barriers

Use: strong forces mean low industry profitability. Decide how to defend or change the forces (build barriers, differentiate, find alternative suppliers). Competitor analysis looks at who the competitors are, their objectives, strategy, strengths and weaknesses, and likely response profile (calm, selective, tiger, stochastic).

4

Internal factors and strategic capability

The resource-based view: competitive advantage comes from resources and competences that rivals cannot easily copy.

Resources and competencesThreshold resources are needed just to compete. Unique resources give advantage. Core competences (Prahalad and Hamel) are a bundle of skills and technology that is valuable to customers, hard to imitate and can be used across products. Resource audit: tangible (plant, cash), intangible (brand, patents, data), human (skills, culture), financial.
Tests of a source of advantage (VRIO)Valuable to customers · Rare · Inimitable (hard to copy) · Organised to be exploited. If all four are met, advantage can be sustained.
Dynamic capabilities (Teece)The ability to sense change, seize opportunities and reconfigure resources fast. Matters in fast-changing markets where fixed resources lose value.
Agile organisationsQuick to sense and respond: flat structure, empowered cross-functional teams, short planning cycles, data-based decisions. Needs the right culture and IT; harder in large regulated firms.

Acquiring capability: build internally, buy (acquire), or partner (alliance, outsource). Judge by speed, cost, control and risk.

Exam traps

A SWOT that is only a list scores badly. Rank the points, quote numbers from the scenario, and end with what the entity should do. Also remember that financial and non-financial information (ratios, customer data, employee turnover) together form the picture of position.

Strategic risk analysis checks risks to the strategy itself (Chapter 7). Business process analysis maps and tests how work flows. Corporate reporting: the annual report and management commentary are sources of information about position and strategy.

6

Levels of strategy

LevelQuestionExample decisions
CorporateWhat business are we in? How do the units fit together?Diversification, acquisitions, portfolio, capital allocation
Business unit (competitive)How do we compete in this market?Cost leadership or differentiation, pricing, target segments
Functional or operationalHow do we deliver the business strategy?Marketing, HR, IT, finance, operations plans

Strategic decisions are long-term, wide and unstructured; operational decisions are short-term, narrow and routine. Strategies at each level must be aligned: a low-cost strategy fails if marketing builds a premium brand.

7

Current technology developments

Big data

Very large, fast, varied data sets (volume, velocity, variety). Value comes from analysis, not storage.

Internet of things

Connected devices and sensors sending data: predictive maintenance, tracking, smart products.

Automation and RPA

Software robots do rule-based tasks: faster, fewer errors, lower cost. Risks: job change, weak process design.

Intelligent systems

AI and machine learning find patterns and make or support decisions. Risks: bias, explainability, data quality.

Distributed ledger and blockchain

Shared, tamper-resistant record without a central owner. Uses: payments, supply chain, smart contracts.

Digital assets and cryptocurrency

Value held in digital form. Volatile, lightly regulated; accounting is judgement (often IAS 38 intangible, or IAS 2 for traders).

Bangladesh note

The manual notes that digital currencies are restricted in Bangladesh by Bangladesh Bank, so a Bangladeshi entity should treat crypto-asset ideas as a high compliance and legal risk.

For each technology answer: opportunity (cost, speed, new products), risk (cyber, data, regulation, skills) and effect on the accountant's role (less routine work, more analysis, assurance over data and algorithms).

8

ESG and sustainability

Sustainability means meeting present needs without harming the future. ESG covers environmental (climate, resources, pollution), social (people, safety, communities, supply-chain labour) and governance (board, ethics, transparency) factors that affect long-term value.

Why it matters strategicallyInvestors and lenders now price ESG risk; customers and employees expect it; regulation is rising; efficiency savings (energy, waste); brand and licence to operate; access to supply chains (global buyers set standards).
Role of chartered accountantsMeasure and report (IFRS S1 and S2, integrated reporting); build ESG into planning and decisions; manage risk and controls; provide assurance to prevent greenwashing; advise on regulation or market solutions (carbon tax, trading, green finance).
Exam focus

Typical requirements: evaluate the external environment for a named company; identify strengths, weaknesses and advise; assess a portfolio; explain how a technology could change the business; discuss ESG risks and reporting. Always finish with a clear recommendation.
