Commercial analysis

Commercial analysis means a full review of what the business does now and where it plans to go, using financial and non-financial information. It covers two areas: (1) financial, operational and strategic analysis, and (2) business trust and ethical awareness. It affects every requirement.

ToolHow to use it in the case
PESTLEPolitical (government, import duties, policy), Economic (inflation, exchange rate, interest), Social (consumers, demographics), Technological, Legal (VAT, BSEC, regulators), Environmental. Give the 3 or 4 factors that matter most and why.

The manual's warning

You will rarely be asked to reproduce a full PESTLE, SWOT or five forces. You are expected to use relevant points from them inside a focused discussion of an operational or strategic issue, and to update them for the new information in the exam (news clips, the investment options).

2

Developing industry awareness

The case gives a note on the industry (Exhibit 4) and a news article. Also build your own knowledge of the Bangladesh economy and key sectors before the exam.

  • Market: size, growth, main players, customers, pricing power, entry barriers.
  • Regulation and tax: import duty and VAT rules, BSEC and DSE listing rules, labour law, environmental clearance, product standards (BSTI), health regulation.
  • Supply chain: imported inputs, exchange rate exposure, dependence on a few suppliers.
  • Technology and trends: automation, e-commerce, renewable energy, changing consumer habits.
  • Known industry problems: counterfeit goods, tax and duty disputes, safety failures, pollution, labour unrest, market syndicates.

Use the industry facts to judge whether the company's results are good for its sector and whether the investment options fit where the market is going.

3

Ethics and business trust

Ethics are the written and unwritten principles and values that govern decisions and actions in an organisation. Business trust is the trust outsiders place in a business. Once lost, it is hard to rebuild. Ethics has two parts: know right from wrong and choose to do right.

The four-way testIs it truthful? Fair to everyone affected? Will it build goodwill? Is it beneficial to all with a stake? If all answers are yes it is probably ethical.
The publicity testHow would the press and social media react if this decision became public? If the organisation would not want it known (apart from commercial secrets), it may be an ethical problem.

Fundamental principles (ICAB and IESBA Codes): integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour. Threats: self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity, intimidation. Ethical issues do not carry a label: look for them in the facts, the news clips and the way the management behaves.

4

Ethical issues in the Case Study

The cover page of the recent papers lists the types of ethical issue the examiners have in mind:

Independence and objectivity

Lack of independence of advisers, auditors or directors; relationships with the client.

Conflict of interest

Between stakeholders (owners, directors, family, lenders, customers, suppliers).

Doubtful accounting or commercial practice

Aggressive revenue, hidden liabilities, unfair competition, market proximity or price-fixing.

Pressure to hit a result

Pressure to show profit for an IPO, a loan, or a bonus.

Compliance with law

Duty and tax underpayment, VAT, labour law, environmental law, product safety.

Sustainability: the four pillars

The manual describes three dimensions (environmental, social and business). Every recent case asks you to assess human, social, economic and environmental sustainability. A business is sustainable when it earns on all four. There is not always a trade-off: good environmental practice can raise reputation and long-term profit.

PillarWhat to look for in the case
HumanTraining and skills; health and safety; wages and conditions; retention and succession; diversity; labour relations; product safety for customers; capability needed for the new plan
SocialBenefit to customers and community; employment and local suppliers; fair prices; access (for example affordable healthcare); donations and CSR; supply chain labour standards; consumer protection; public health
EconomicProfitability and long-term viability; contribution to jobs, tax, exports and local value-added; investment and growth; dependence on imports or one customer; resilience to shocks
EnvironmentalEnergy and water use; emissions; waste and recycling; pollution and clean-up liabilities; raw materials (recycled or virgin); climate risk; environmental compliance and certification

Layout: for each pillar give 3 or 4 points (strength first, then gap), end with a conclusion (is the business sustainable?) and a recommendation (for example "adopt an environmental policy and monitor emissions before the IPO"). Link the new investment option to each pillar. Also note that a prospectus and IFRS S1 and S2 style reporting will expose sustainability gaps.

Exam focus

Requirement (c) is usually (i) ethical issues and (ii) four-pillar sustainability, worth about a third of the report. Use Exhibit 7 (the industry article) and Exhibit 8 (news clips) heavily; they contain the clues. Always end each part with a conclusion and recommendation.
