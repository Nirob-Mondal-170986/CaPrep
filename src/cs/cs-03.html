NPV with a CAPM discount rate

In the past cases the board says: mutually exclusive options, discount rate by CAPM with the 10-year government bond coupon as the risk-free rate, tax as given (or disregarded unless stated), and cash flows at year ends after the initial outflow.

CAPM rate = risk-free rate + beta × (market return − risk-free rate) Free cash flow = operating profit after tax (or revenue − direct and operating costs − tax) − capital expenditure − working capital increase Terminal value (growth g after the last year) = last-year cash flow × (1 + g) ÷ (discount rate − g)
Check from the Luna case

Risk-free 10.8%, market return 12.4%, beta 1.35.

CAPM = 10.8% + 1.35 × (12.4% − 10.8%) = 10.8% + 2.16%12.96% ≈ 13%
Year 5 free cash flow 201,561,500; growth 4%; terminal value = 201,561,500 × 1.04 ÷ (0.13 − 0.04)2,329,155,111
NPV of the manufacturing option (official answer)+382,780,475
NPV of the e-commerce acquisition (official answer)(23,805,117)

The board chose by NPV and by compatibility with the mission and vision. The positive-NPV option also fitted the mission (own quality control and local production), so it was recommended.

Small example with sensitivity

Outlay 1,000 now; free cash flow 300 at the end of each of 5 years; discount rate 13%.

Annuity factor at 13% for 5 years3.517
PV of cash flows 300 × 3.5171,055
NPV (1,055 − 1,000)55
Simple payback 1,000 ÷ 3003.3 years
IRR (rate where NPV = 0)about 15.2%
Margin: cash flows can fall by 55 ÷ 1,0555.2% before NPV = 0

A small positive NPV with only a 5% margin means the decision is sensitive. Flex cash flows, the discount rate and the terminal growth, and say whether the conclusion changes.

  • Do the calculation as the board defined it first; then challenge assumptions (growth of 25% a year, constant margins, no working capital) and show the effect.
  • Terminal value often drives the NPV: state its share of the total and test a lower growth rate.
  • Do not rely on NPV alone: compare with mission and vision, risk, capability, stakeholders, funding, ESG and ethics.
  • For an acquisition: price against value to the acquirer, synergy, integration cost, due diligence, and cash needed.
3

Quality of data, assumptions and flexing the numbers

Provenance and accuracyUse the 7 Ps (Chapter 1). Who prepared the forecast, for what purpose, how far ahead? A forecast by the proposer of the project is likely to be optimistic. Decide how accurate the answer needs to be: do not spend time on precision if the data is rough.
Evaluating assumptionsList each key assumption (revenue growth, margin, capital cost, tax, discount rate, timing). Say which are reasonable, which are aggressive and why. Missing items: working capital, maintenance, licences, marketing, inflation, exchange rates.
FlexingChange one variable at a time and find the break-even value for the decision; then try a realistic downside scenario. Present as a small table.
Reconciliations and errorsSpreadsheets in the case may contain errors. Check totals, signs, units (thousands or millions), timing (year 0 or year 1), double counting and that the figures agree to the financial statements.
Interpretation

Always finish a calculation with a sentence: "The NPV of BDT 383 million is positive, but the present value of the terminal value (about BDT 1,264 million) is more than three times the NPV, so the decision depends on the 4% long-term growth assumption." That sentence earns SP&S and AJ marks; the number alone earns only A&UI.

Financing and valuation calculations used in the cases

DecisionMethod
Is a bank loan viable?Build the project cash flows after loan interest, tax and repayments, including any grace period, penalty rates and covenants (such as debt to equity below 4:1). Compute NPV or check that cash covers debt service each year. Negative NPV or an unfunded year means not viable.
IPO share premium acceptable to BSEC?Estimate a price three ways (NAV, EPS, sector P/E), average them with equal weight, and compare with the price the board proposes (see Chapter 7).
Number of shares to issueFunds needed ÷ (1 − issue cost %) ÷ issue price; then ownership after the issue = old shares ÷ total shares, against the board's limit.
EPS and dilutionProfit ÷ weighted shares before and after the issue; diluted EPS includes new shares; comment on dilution for existing shareholders.
Sale and leasebackCash received now less PV of lease rentals (and tax effects); compare with borrowing; consider renewal risk and land price growth.
Venture capital or share offerNew shares needed = funds ÷ price; ownership given up; enterprise value by NPV compared with the offer price per share.
Share exchange ratio in an acquisitionBy book value (NAV per share of each company) and by discounted cash flow (value per share of each); choose the ratio that is better for your shareholders, check EPS dilution, state the incremental risks of cash flow estimates.
