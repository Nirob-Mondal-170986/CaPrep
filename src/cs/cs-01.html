1

What the Case Study is

The Case Study tests whether you can give advice on complex business issues in a written report. You play a real role, for example a manager at a firm of chartered accountants advising the board of a company. You get an email from the client, background notes on the business and industry, draft financial statements, emails with accounting and investment issues, an article on ethics and the industry, and news clips.

  • All marks are for skills, not facts. You do not score by copying the case. You score by analysing it, using judgement and recommending.
  • One output only: a report with an Executive Summary and answers to three requirements (a), (b), (c).
  • Calculations are lighter than in the other Advanced Level papers but you must do financial and business analysis.
  • Real situations: in the past cases the client is a Bangladeshi company (a TV importer, plastic maker, hospital, supermarket, ceramics firm, pet care firm, drinking water, cinema, paper, e-commerce, renewable energy, agro business, restaurants).
  • Ethics, business trust and sustainability run through every case. Professional scepticism is expected throughout.

Typical allocation from the manual. The recent past papers show the same idea with an integrative and multidisciplinary column (for example 25 / 25 / 20 / 20 / 10 across the whole paper) and the Executive Summary at 15 marks.

  • Equal weight: the three requirements are worth about the same. Spend similar time on each. Not attempting one, or leaving out the Executive Summary, badly hurts your chance of passing.
  • All four skills are assessed in every requirement. Analysis without judgement, or judgement without a clear recommendation, loses marks.
  • Ethics: about 10% of the marks are for relevant discussion of ethical issues (stated in the cover page of recent papers).
  • Numbers: a calculation earns marks for the correct method and its use in the report, not only for the final figure. Show workings in annexures and refer to them.
  • Overall criteria: report structure (from the firm, disclaimer, headings) and language (formal, tactful, ethical).
3

The four professional skills

Assimilating and Using Information (A&UI)

Find and use the relevant data from the exhibits; do the basic calculations; spot the issues and the wider context; notice ethical and trust issues.

Structuring Problems and Solutions (SP&S)

Analyse the figures and facts; explain causes and effects; comment on assumptions; make adjustments; sensitivity analysis; show merits and demerits.

Applying Judgement (AJ)

Evaluate the analysis and options: question assumptions, spot bias and inconsistency, weigh stakeholders, ethics and sustainability. The skill most candidates find hardest.

Conclusions and Recommendations (C&R)

A clear decision from the analysis, with reasons, concerns, financial effect, extra work needed and next steps.

How A&UI is shownUse specific facts and numbers from the exhibits; include the correct calculations from the information; show you understand the industry and the stage of the business; identify what is missing.
How SP&S is shownCalculate and explain the result; state the cause (because...); show positive and negative points; link ethics to the issue; test the assumptions by flexing the numbers.
How AJ is shown"On balance...", "however...", "this depends on..."; compare options against criteria; challenge the data quality; weigh the interests of stakeholders; consider what could go wrong. Judgement is the bridge between analysis and recommendation.
How C&R is shownState the decision in one sentence, justify it, list the main concerns, give the money effect of acting and of not acting, and name the next steps. Offer a clear choice, not a list of options.
4

Professional scepticism: the 7 Ps

Never accept information at face value. Ask these seven questions about any data in the case (including figures produced by a computer system).

PAsk
PreparerWho wrote it? Insider with a vested interest or an outsider? How skilled?
PurposeWhy was it written? To get a loan, justify a bonus or an investment? Any bias?
PrecisionHow accurate: audited, draft, management accounts, forecast, extrapolation?
PredictionsAre the time frame and assumptions realistic? The further ahead, the less reliable.
ProblemsWhat are the big and small problems? Financial or non-financial? Short or long term?
PriorityHow urgent and important is each issue?
PerspectiveWhose view: the preparer, the board, the shareholders, the regulator? Who is the audience?

Always identify and question the assumptions given (growth rates, discount rates, costs). Also note missing information (an expense in one exhibit that does not appear in another) and say what extra information you would ask for.

Critical success factors

  • Apply what you know from the earlier modules (reporting, finance, audit, strategy, ethics). Choose the right tool for the issue; the paper rarely names it.
  • Know where the business is in its life cycle (start-up, growth, maturity, decline) and its generic problems.
  • Know your role. You are an adviser: independent, objective, giving clear advice. Not a manager of the company.
  • Quality, not quantity. A focused, reasoned answer beats a long list. Run out of time rather than space.
  • Make the numbers talk (explain what the figures mean for this business).
  • Judgement and recommendation in every part. Never stop at analysis.
  • Practise whole cases under time conditions. Judgement cannot be learned in the last weeks.
Where the marks usually go

Candidates pass by covering all three requirements and the Executive Summary, with specific facts and numbers, an evaluation (not just description) and a decision. They fail by copying the case, giving generic answers, ignoring ethics, or over-investing in one requirement.
